# Disease Prevention

## Topic
Disease Prevention

## Overview

Plant disease prevention focuses on reducing the chance that pathogens become established, multiply, and spread through a crop.

Plant diseases can be caused by:
- Fungi
- Oomycetes
- Bacteria
- Viruses
- Viroids
- Nematodes
- Other plant pathogens

Disease prevention is generally more effective and less costly than attempting to control severe disease after extensive infection has occurred.

Important prevention principles include:
- Starting with healthy planting material.
- Choosing resistant or tolerant varieties where available.
- Maintaining field sanitation.
- Managing water and humidity.
- Improving airflow.
- Practicing crop rotation.
- Controlling weeds and volunteer plants.
- Monitoring crops regularly.
- Preventing movement of infected material.

## Disease Triangle

Disease generally requires three components:

### Host
A susceptible plant must be present.

### Pathogen
A viable disease-causing organism or infectious agent must be present.

### Environment
Environmental conditions must favor infection, development, or spread.

Disease prevention can target any part of this triangle:
- Reduce host susceptibility.
- Reduce pathogen inoculum.
- Make environmental conditions less favorable for disease.

## Healthy Planting Material

Disease-free planting material is one of the most important preventive measures.

Use:
- Certified seed where available.
- Tested seed where appropriate.
- Healthy transplants.
- Disease-free cuttings or grafting material.
- Reliable nursery sources.

Inspect planting material for:
- Leaf lesions
- Cankers
- Root abnormalities
- Wilting
- Mosaic symptoms
- Insect vectors
- Poor vigor

Avoid introducing visibly diseased plants into a healthy production area.

## Resistant Varieties

Plant varieties can differ significantly in susceptibility to disease.

Where available:
- Select resistant varieties for important local diseases.
- Prefer varieties with documented resistance to locally relevant pathogen populations.
- Consider resistance together with agronomic performance and market requirements.

Resistance does not always provide complete protection.

Some pathogens can:
- Overcome specific resistance.
- Develop new strains or races.
- Cause disease under high inoculum pressure.

Resistant varieties should therefore be combined with other preventive practices.

## Crop Rotation

Crop rotation can reduce disease pressure by breaking pathogen life cycles.

Rotation can help when pathogens:
- Survive primarily in soil.
- Survive in crop residues.
- Depend strongly on a particular crop host.

Effective rotation depends on pathogen biology.

Rotating between two crops that share the same pathogen host range may provide little benefit.

## Field Sanitation

Sanitation reduces sources of pathogen inoculum.

Useful practices include:
- Removing infected plant material.
- Removing diseased crop residues where appropriate.
- Removing mummified fruit.
- Removing severely diseased plants.
- Controlling volunteer crops.
- Cleaning tools and equipment.
- Keeping production areas clean.

Sanitation should be based on the pathogen because some organisms survive in soil, roots, seeds, residues, or perennial tissues.

## Water Management

Water is important for many plant diseases.

Disease risk may increase with:
- Prolonged leaf wetness.
- Frequent rainfall.
- Overhead irrigation.
- Poor drainage.
- Waterlogged soil.
- High humidity.

Where practical:
- Prefer root-zone irrigation.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Irrigate when foliage can dry quickly.
- Improve drainage.
- Avoid prolonged soil saturation.

Not every pathogen requires free water, so water-management recommendations should be crop- and disease-specific.

## Airflow and Canopy Management

Dense canopies can:
- Increase humidity.
- Extend leaf-wetness duration.
- Reduce air movement.
- Increase disease development.

Canopy-management practices may include:
- Appropriate plant spacing.
- Pruning.
- Shoot positioning.
- Staking.
- Trellising.
- Removal of excessive foliage where appropriate.

These practices can help foliage dry more quickly.

## Weed Management

Some weeds can act as:
- Alternative pathogen hosts.
- Insect-vector hosts.
- Sources of inoculum.

Controlling relevant weeds can therefore reduce disease risk.

Weed management should focus particularly on weeds known to host pathogens or vectors important to the crop.

## Plant Nutrition

Balanced nutrition supports healthy plant development.

Nutrient deficiencies or excesses can:
- Reduce plant vigor.
- Alter tissue susceptibility.
- Increase stress.
- Produce symptoms that resemble disease.

Excessive nitrogen may produce soft, succulent growth that can increase susceptibility to some diseases.

Nutrition should therefore be based on:
- Soil testing.
- Crop requirements.
- Plant analysis where appropriate.
- Local recommendations.

## Pest and Vector Management

Insect pests can contribute to disease by:
- Creating wounds.
- Moving pathogens between plants.
- Acting as vectors for viruses and other pathogens.

Disease-prevention programs should therefore monitor important vectors such as:
- Whiteflies
- Aphids
- Thrips
- Leafhoppers
- Psyllids
- Other crop-specific vectors

Vector management should be integrated with crop monitoring and current pest-management recommendations.

## Tool and Equipment Sanitation

Some pathogens spread mechanically through:
- Pruning knives.
- Scissors.
- Cutting equipment.
- Harvest tools.
- Trays.
- Containers.
- Gloves.
- Hands.
- Machinery.

Important practices include:
- Cleaning tools between infected and healthy areas.
- Sanitizing reusable equipment according to appropriate procedures.
- Avoiding unnecessary movement of contaminated plant material.
- Handling healthy plants before visibly diseased plants when practical.

Mechanical sanitation is particularly important for diseases that spread through infected sap.

## Seed and Transplant Hygiene

Disease can be introduced into a field through:
- Infected seed.
- Contaminated transplant trays.
- Infected nursery plants.
- Contaminated propagation media.
- Grafting material.

Nursery prevention should include:
- Clean seed.
- Clean trays and containers.
- Clean propagation areas.
- Inspection of seedlings.
- Removal of symptomatic plants.
- Prevention of cross-contamination.

## Greenhouse and Protected Cultivation

Disease risk can increase in greenhouses and high tunnels because:
- Humidity may remain high.
- Air circulation may be limited.
- Crops are grown densely.
- The same crop may be grown repeatedly.

Important preventive practices include:
- Ventilation.
- Air circulation.
- Humidity management.
- Clean planting material.
- Sanitation.
- Removal of infected plants.
- Cleaning structures and equipment between crops.

## Monitoring and Early Detection

Regular scouting is essential.

Monitor:
- Young leaves.
- Lower leaves.
- New shoots.
- Flowers.
- Fruit.
- Stems.
- Roots where appropriate.

Look for:
- Spots.
- Wilting.
- Yellowing.
- Cankers.
- Mold.
- Pustules.
- Rot.
- Mosaic patterns.
- Distortion.
- Unusual growth.

Early detection allows preventive or targeted action before disease spreads widely.

## Disease Forecasting

Some diseases can be predicted more effectively using:
- Temperature.
- Rainfall.
- Relative humidity.
- Leaf-wetness duration.
- Crop growth stage.
- Disease history.

Where disease-warning systems are available, they can support:
- Scouting intensity.
- Timing of preventive measures.
- Fungicide decisions.
- Irrigation adjustments.

Forecasting should complement direct field observations rather than replace them.

## Quarantine and Plant Movement

Some diseases can spread between regions through movement of:
- Plants.
- Seedlings.
- Cuttings.
- Grafting material.
- Soil.
- Machinery.
- Fruit or other infected plant products.

Applicable quarantine rules should be followed when moving agricultural material.

Do not move suspected infected plants from an affected area without checking applicable plant-health regulations.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select healthy planting material.
- Use certified seed where available.
- Select resistant or tolerant varieties.
- Review field disease history.
- Practice suitable crop rotation.
- Remove volunteer host plants.
- Plan irrigation and drainage.
- Prepare clean propagation areas.

### During Growth
- Scout regularly.
- Monitor weather and disease-risk conditions.
- Maintain balanced nutrition.
- Improve canopy airflow.
- Avoid unnecessary leaf wetness.
- Control relevant weeds.
- Remove diseased material when appropriate.
- Sanitize tools and equipment.
- Manage insect vectors.
- Follow crop-specific disease-management recommendations.

### After Harvest
- Remove crop residues where appropriate.
- Remove diseased fruit and plant material.
- Control volunteer plants.
- Clean tools and equipment.
- Sanitize greenhouse or nursery facilities.
- Review disease outbreaks.
- Record management results for future seasons.

## Biological / Organic Management

Biological and nonchemical prevention can include:
- Resistant varieties.
- Beneficial microorganisms where supported by evidence.
- Biological control of insect vectors.
- Crop rotation.
- Sanitation.
- Cultural management.
- Physical barriers.
- Healthy planting material.

Biological products should be selected according to:
- Crop.
- Pathogen.
- Production system.
- Current evidence.
- Local regulatory approval.

## Chemical Management

Preventive fungicides, bactericides, or vector-control products may be appropriate for some crops and diseases.

Chemical management should be based on:
- Confirmed or well-supported disease risk.
- Crop growth stage.
- Weather conditions.
- Pathogen biology.
- Disease history.
- Local recommendations.

Preventive treatments generally provide greater value when disease risk is high and infection has not yet become extensive.

Repeated use of the same mode of action can contribute to resistance. Resistance-management practices should therefore be incorporated into chemical programs.

**Important:** Chemical products, permitted crops, formulations, application rates, resistance-management requirements, pre-harvest intervals, and regulations vary by country and can change over time. ## Immediate Actions

When disease is suspected:

1. Isolate or mark affected plants where practical.
2. Identify the crop and affected plant part.
3. Record recent weather and irrigation conditions.
4. Inspect nearby plants for similar symptoms.
5. Look for signs of the pathogen or insect vector.
6. Avoid unnecessary handling of affected plants.
7. Sanitize tools after working with suspected infected material.
8. Remove severely infected material where appropriate.
9. Avoid moving potentially infected plants between production areas.
10. Seek laboratory or agricultural expert confirmation when diagnosis is uncertain.
