# Post-Harvest Handling

## Topic
Post-Harvest Handling

## Overview

Post-harvest handling includes the activities performed after a crop is harvested to maintain quality, reduce losses, and preserve market value.

Important post-harvest activities include:
- Harvesting at the appropriate maturity stage
- Sorting
- Cleaning
- Grading
- Cooling where appropriate
- Packaging
- Transportation
- Storage
- Monitoring for spoilage

Poor handling can cause:
- Bruising
- Cutting and crushing
- Water loss
- Rot
- Mold development
- Physiological disorders
- Quality loss
- Reduced shelf life
- Food safety risks

Post-harvest practices should be adapted to the specific crop and intended market.

## Harvest Timing

Harvest timing should be based on:
- Crop maturity
- Intended use
- Variety
- Market requirements
- Storage requirements
- Transportation distance
- Expected weather

Harvesting too early may result in:
- Poor flavor
- Low size
- Incomplete color development
- Lower nutritional or eating quality

Harvesting too late may result in:
- Over-ripening
- Increased bruising
- Cracking
- Increased disease and decay
- Reduced storage life

## Harvest Conditions

Where practical:
- Harvest during cool, dry conditions.
- Avoid harvesting when crops are excessively wet.
- Avoid unnecessary handling during very high temperatures.
- Use clean harvesting containers.
- Handle produce gently.

Wet produce can be more susceptible to physical damage and microbial decay during storage.

## Hygiene

Clean handling is important for maintaining quality and food safety.

Important practices include:
- Keep hands and equipment clean.
- Clean and sanitize reusable harvest containers where appropriate.
- Keep harvested produce away from contaminated soil and waste.
- Remove visibly rotten or heavily diseased produce.
- Keep storage areas clean.
- Protect produce from animals and other contamination sources.

## Sorting

Sorting removes:
- Damaged produce
- Diseased produce
- Rotten produce
- Severely bruised produce
- Overripe produce
- Undersized or unsuitable produce

Removing damaged produce can reduce the spread of decay during storage because damaged tissues often become entry points for pathogens.

## Grading

Grading may be based on:
- Size
- Color
- Shape
- Maturity
- Weight
- Surface quality
- Freedom from defects

Grading requirements depend on:
- Crop
- Market
- Buyer
- Processing requirements
- Applicable standards

## Cleaning

Some crops may require cleaning after harvest to remove:
- Soil
- Plant debris
- Foreign material

Cleaning methods should be appropriate for the crop.

Excessive washing can sometimes increase storage problems if produce remains wet and is not properly dried.

Cleaning and sanitation procedures must distinguish between removing visible dirt and ensuring microbial food safety.

## Cooling

Cooling after harvest can slow:
- Respiration
- Water loss
- Softening
- Aging
- Some forms of pathogen development

Methods may include:
- Room cooling
- Forced-air cooling
- Hydro-cooling
- Ice cooling
- Vacuum cooling

Not every crop is suitable for every cooling method.

The correct cooling method depends on:
- Crop physiology
- Temperature sensitivity
- Product size
- Packaging
- Available infrastructure

## Temperature Management

Temperature is one of the most important factors affecting post-harvest quality.

Lower temperatures generally slow respiration and aging, but some crops are sensitive to chilling injury.

Chilling-sensitive crops can develop:
- Surface pitting
- Poor color development
- Browning
- Abnormal ripening
- Increased decay

Storage temperature should therefore be crop-specific rather than universally minimized.

## Relative Humidity

Relative humidity affects water loss and decay.

Very low humidity can increase:
- Wilting
- Shriveling
- Weight loss
- Loss of firmness

Very high humidity can increase:
- Condensation
- Surface wetness
- Mold development
- Some forms of decay

The suitable humidity range depends on the crop.

## Packaging

Good packaging should:
- Protect produce from physical damage.
- Allow appropriate ventilation where required.
- Reduce moisture loss when appropriate.
- Facilitate handling and transportation.
- Maintain required product quality.

Packaging should not trap excessive condensation.

Packaging materials should be clean and suitable for the crop and intended market.

## Transportation

During transport:
- Avoid excessive vibration.
- Avoid crushing.
- Maintain suitable temperature.
- Protect produce from direct sunlight.
- Avoid unnecessary delays.
- Maintain clean transport conditions.

Poor transportation can cause mechanical injury that increases post-harvest decay.

## Storage

Storage conditions should be matched to the crop.

Important factors include:
- Temperature
- Relative humidity
- Ventilation
- Ethylene exposure
- Product maturity
- Air circulation
- Packaging
- Sanitation

Storage areas should be inspected regularly for:
- Rot
- Mold
- Shriveling
- Condensation
- Pest activity
- Temperature problems

## Ethylene

Ethylene is a plant hormone involved in:
- Ripening
- Senescence
- Abscission

Some crops produce substantial quantities of ethylene, while others are highly sensitive to it.

Ethylene-sensitive produce can experience:
- Premature ripening
- Yellowing
- Softening
- Loss of quality

Where relevant, ethylene-producing and ethylene-sensitive crops should be separated during storage.

## Post-Harvest Diseases

Many pathogens can continue developing after harvest.

Decay is promoted by:
- Bruising
- Cuts
- Insect injury
- Excessive moisture
- High temperature
- Poor sanitation
- Infected produce stored with healthy produce

Important prevention methods include:
- Gentle handling
- Sorting
- Sanitation
- Appropriate storage temperature
- Appropriate humidity
- Removal of infected produce

## Mechanical Damage

Mechanical damage includes:
- Bruising
- Cuts
- Compression
- Abrasion
- Punctures

Damage can:
- Reduce appearance.
- Accelerate water loss.
- Increase respiration.
- Create entry points for pathogens.
- Reduce storage life.

Harvest containers should therefore be designed and handled to minimize impact and compression.

## Food Safety

Post-harvest produce should be handled according to applicable food-safety requirements.

Important practices include:
- Clean water for produce washing when washing is used.
- Clean harvesting equipment.
- Hygienic worker practices.
- Protection from animal contamination.
- Separation of contaminated and clean produce.
- Appropriate storage and transport conditions.

Food-safety requirements vary by crop, market, processing status, and jurisdiction.

## Management

### Before Harvest
- Determine appropriate maturity indicators.
- Prepare clean harvest containers.
- Prepare storage facilities.
- Confirm cooling and transportation arrangements.
- Review market requirements.
- Check weather conditions before harvest.

### During Harvest
- Harvest at the appropriate maturity stage.
- Handle produce gently.
- Avoid dropping or throwing produce.
- Keep harvested produce out of direct sun where practical.
- Separate damaged or diseased produce.
- Use clean containers and equipment.

### After Harvest
- Sort and grade produce.
- Clean when appropriate.
- Cool products when appropriate.
- Store under crop-specific conditions.
- Monitor temperature and humidity.
- Inspect regularly for decay.
- Remove deteriorating produce.
- Maintain storage sanitation.
- Transport using suitable handling and temperature practices.

## Crop-Specific Considerations

Post-harvest requirements vary significantly.

### Fruits
Important factors often include:
- Ripeness
- Firmness
- Temperature
- Ethylene sensitivity
- Bruising

### Vegetables
Important factors often include:
- Water loss
- Turgidity
- Temperature
- Physical damage
- Decay

### Roots and Tubers
Important factors often include:
- Skin condition
- Curing where appropriate
- Temperature
- Humidity
- Sprouting
- Rot

### Grains and Seeds
Important factors often include:
- Moisture content
- Drying
- Pest protection
- Storage temperature
- Mold prevention

## Immediate Actions

When post-harvest quality is deteriorating:

1. Identify the affected crop and maturity stage.
2. Inspect for bruising, cuts, rot, mold, or dehydration.
3. Check storage temperature.
4. Check relative humidity where relevant.
5. Check for condensation or excessive surface moisture.
6. Remove visibly rotten or severely damaged produce.
7. Improve ventilation where appropriate.
8. Review packaging and stacking practices.
9. Check transport or handling damage.
10. Consult crop-specific post-harvest guidance for corrective action.

## Prevention

- Harvest at the appropriate maturity stage.
- Handle produce gently.
- Keep harvesting and storage equipment clean.
- Minimize bruising and cuts.
- Sort damaged and diseased produce promptly.
- Cool appropriate crops quickly.
- Maintain crop-specific storage temperature.
- Maintain suitable humidity.
- Provide appropriate ventilation.
- Use suitable packaging.
- Avoid excessive stacking pressure.
- Monitor stored produce regularly.
- Remove deteriorating produce promptly.
- Maintain hygienic handling and transport conditions.
