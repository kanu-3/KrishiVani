# Fertilization

## Topic
Fertilization

## Overview

Fertilization is the management of plant nutrients through fertilizers and other nutrient sources to support healthy crop growth and production.

Effective fertilization should:
- Supply nutrients required by the crop.
- Correct confirmed or likely nutrient deficiencies.
- Maintain soil fertility.
- Avoid nutrient excess.
- Minimize nutrient losses.
- Support efficient fertilizer use.

Fertilizer requirements vary with:
- Crop species.
- Variety or hybrid.
- Soil type.
- Soil nutrient status.
- Soil pH.
- Expected yield.
- Previous crop.
- Organic matter.
- Irrigation.
- Growth stage.
- Local climate.

There is no single fertilizer program suitable for every crop or field.

## Essential Nutrients

Plants require:

### Macronutrients
- Nitrogen (N)
- Phosphorus (P)
- Potassium (K)
- Calcium (Ca)
- Magnesium (Mg)
- Sulfur (S)

### Micronutrients
- Iron (Fe)
- Manganese (Mn)
- Zinc (Zn)
- Copper (Cu)
- Boron (B)
- Molybdenum (Mo)
- Chlorine (Cl)
- Nickel (Ni)

The correct nutrient balance is more important than simply applying larger quantities of fertilizer.

## Soil Testing

Soil testing is one of the most useful tools for developing a fertilization plan.

Testing can provide information about:
- Soil pH.
- Available phosphorus.
- Available potassium.
- Other nutrients depending on the test.
- Organic matter.
- Salinity or electrical conductivity where included.

A representative soil sample should be collected using appropriate sampling procedures for the crop and field.

Fertilizer applications should preferably be based on soil-test results together with crop-specific recommendations.

## Nitrogen Fertilization

Nitrogen is important for:
- Vegetative growth.
- Chlorophyll production.
- Photosynthesis.
- Protein synthesis.

Too little nitrogen can cause:
- Older leaves to become pale or yellow.
- Reduced growth.
- Small leaves.
- Reduced crop vigor.

Too much nitrogen can cause:
- Excessive vegetative growth.
- Delayed maturity in some crops.
- Soft, succulent tissue.
- Increased susceptibility to some pests and diseases.
- Poor reproductive development in some crops.

Nitrogen is often split into multiple applications when appropriate to reduce losses and better match crop demand.

## Phosphorus Fertilization

Phosphorus supports:
- Root development.
- Energy transfer.
- Flowering.
- Seed development.
- Early establishment.

Phosphorus fertilizer should be applied according to soil-test results and crop requirements.

Excess phosphorus can:
- Accumulate in soil.
- Increase environmental losses.
- Interfere with availability of some micronutrients, particularly zinc.

## Potassium Fertilization

Potassium supports:
- Water regulation.
- Stomatal function.
- Enzyme activity.
- Carbohydrate transport.
- Stress tolerance.
- Fruit, tuber, and grain development.

Potassium deficiency may cause:
- Yellowing or browning of leaf margins.
- Leaf-edge scorch.
- Reduced vigor.
- Poor crop development.

Excessive potassium can interfere with uptake of other cations such as magnesium and calcium.

## Secondary Nutrients

### Calcium
Calcium supports:
- Cell walls.
- Membrane stability.
- Root development.
- Young tissue development.

Calcium supply is strongly influenced by root health and water availability.

### Magnesium
Magnesium is a central component of chlorophyll and supports photosynthesis.

Deficiency often appears as interveinal chlorosis on older leaves.

### Sulfur
Sulfur is required for:
- Protein synthesis.
- Enzyme function.
- Formation of sulfur-containing compounds.

Deficiency often affects younger tissues.

## Micronutrient Fertilization

Micronutrients are required in relatively small amounts.

Important micronutrients include:
- Iron
- Zinc
- Manganese
- Copper
- Boron
- Molybdenum

Micronutrient applications should be made carefully because the difference between adequate and excessive concentrations can be relatively small for some nutrients.

Micronutrient fertilizers should generally be used when:
- Soil tests indicate deficiency.
- Plant tissue tests indicate deficiency.
- Crop-specific recommendations identify a likely deficiency.
- Local conditions make deficiency likely.

## Fertilizer Sources

Fertilizers can be supplied through:

### Mineral Fertilizers
Examples include:
- Urea.
- Ammonium-based fertilizers.
- Phosphate fertilizers.
- Potassium fertilizers.
- Compound or blended fertilizers.

### Organic Nutrient Sources
Examples include:
- Compost.
- Properly managed manure.
- Crop residues.
- Green manure.
- Other approved organic amendments.

Nutrient concentration varies greatly among organic materials and should be considered before application.

## Fertilizer Placement

Methods include:
- Broadcast application.
- Band application.
- Side-dressing.
- Basal application.
- Foliar application.
- Fertigation.

The appropriate method depends on:
- Crop.
- Nutrient.
- Soil.
- Fertilizer type.
- Growth stage.
- Irrigation system.

Some fertilizers can injure seeds if placed directly against them at excessive concentration.

## Fertilization Timing

Nutrients should be supplied when crops can use them effectively.

Timing depends on:
- Crop growth stage.
- Nutrient mobility.
- Soil properties.
- Irrigation.
- Weather.

Nitrogen is often applied in split doses because it is relatively mobile in soil and can be lost through:
- Leaching.
- Volatilization.
- Other pathways depending on conditions.

## Fertigation

Fertigation is the application of dissolved nutrients through irrigation systems.

Potential advantages include:
- More precise nutrient delivery.
- Smaller and repeated applications.
- Improved synchronization with crop demand.

Risks include:
- Over-fertilization.
- Uneven application.
- Irrigation-system blockage.
- Chemical incompatibility.
- Root or leaf injury if concentrations are excessive.

Fertigation programs should be designed specifically for the crop and irrigation system.

## Foliar Fertilization

Foliar fertilizers are applied directly to leaves.

They may be useful for:
- Certain micronutrient deficiencies.
- Situations where rapid correction is needed.
- Crops with specific foliar-feeding recommendations.

Foliar applications should not automatically replace soil-based nutrient management.

Leaf injury can occur if:
- Concentrations are excessive.
- Weather is unsuitable.
- Products are incompatible.
- Leaves are stressed.

## Fertilization and Soil pH

Soil pH influences nutrient availability.

A soil may contain adequate total nutrient quantities while the nutrient remains poorly available because of unsuitable pH.

Before correcting suspected nutrient deficiency:
- Check soil pH.
- Review soil-test results.
- Consider root health.
- Consider irrigation and drainage.

Correcting pH may improve nutrient availability without adding large amounts of additional fertilizer.

## Nutrient Interactions

Nutrients can interact with one another.

Examples include:
- Excess phosphorus reducing zinc availability.
- Excess potassium interfering with magnesium uptake.
- Nutrient imbalance affecting calcium availability.
- Excess fertilizer increasing salinity.

Applying more of one nutrient does not necessarily correct a deficiency if the underlying problem is caused by another factor.

## Fertilization and Water

Adequate water is necessary for nutrient uptake.

Poor irrigation can cause:
- Reduced nutrient uptake during drought.
- Nutrient leaching under excessive irrigation.
- Root damage under waterlogging.
- Uneven nutrient distribution.

Fertilizer and irrigation programs should therefore be planned together.

## Fertilization and Disease

Excessive nitrogen can produce soft, vigorous tissue that may increase susceptibility to some pests and diseases.

Balanced nutrition generally supports:
- Strong plant growth.
- Better root development.
- Improved stress tolerance.

Nutrient management should not be used as a substitute for disease diagnosis and disease-specific management.

## Fertilization and Salinity

Repeated fertilizer applications can contribute to soluble salt accumulation, particularly where:
- Irrigation water is saline.
- Drainage is poor.
- Fertilizer applications are excessive.

High salinity can cause:
- Poor germination.
- Root stress.
- Leaf-edge burn.
- Reduced water uptake.
- Reduced crop growth.

Soil and water testing can help identify salinity problems.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Conduct a soil test.
- Measure soil pH.
- Review previous crop history.
- Estimate crop nutrient demand.
- Select fertilizer sources.
- Calculate nutrient requirements based on crop and soil information.
- Correct major soil limitations where appropriate.

### During Growth
- Monitor crop development.
- Observe possible nutrient-deficiency symptoms.
- Use soil or tissue testing when needed.
- Split fertilizer applications where appropriate.
- Match nutrient supply to crop demand.
- Avoid unnecessary fertilizer applications.
- Monitor irrigation and drainage.
- Record fertilizer applications.

### After Harvest
- Review crop yield.
- Review fertilizer-use efficiency.
- Consider nutrient removal by the harvested crop.
- Repeat soil testing according to local recommendations.
- Adjust future fertilizer plans based on results.
- Maintain organic matter and appropriate nutrient balance.

## Fertilizer Application Safety

Before applying fertilizer:
- Read the product label.
- Confirm the crop and permitted use.
- Follow recommended application rates.
- Wear appropriate protective equipment where required.
- Keep fertilizer away from children and animals.
- Store fertilizer in a dry, secure location.
- Avoid contamination of drinking water.
- Prevent fertilizer spills from entering water bodies.

## Immediate Actions

When a nutrient deficiency is suspected:

1. Identify which leaves or plant parts are affected.
2. Determine whether symptoms appear on older or younger tissues.
3. Review recent fertilizer applications.
4. Check irrigation and drainage.
5. Inspect root-zone conditions.
6. Conduct a soil test where appropriate.
7. Consider plant tissue testing for difficult cases.
8. Check soil pH.
9. Avoid applying large amounts of fertilizer without confirming the likely cause.
10. Consult a local agricultural expert for crop-specific correction recommendations.

## Prevention

- Test soil regularly.
- Base fertilizer decisions on soil and crop requirements.
- Maintain suitable soil pH.
- Use balanced nutrient programs.
- Split nutrient applications where appropriate.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen.
- Avoid unnecessary micronutrient applications.
- Maintain suitable irrigation and drainage.
- Monitor crop growth.
- Keep accurate fertilizer records.
- Review fertilizer performance after harvest.
