# Irrigation

## Topic
Irrigation

## Overview

Irrigation is the controlled application of water to crops when rainfall is insufficient to meet plant water requirements.

Good irrigation management aims to:
- Maintain adequate root-zone moisture.
- Support healthy crop growth.
- Reduce drought stress.
- Avoid waterlogging.
- Improve water-use efficiency.
- Reduce nutrient losses.
- Maintain crop yield and quality.

Irrigation requirements vary with:
- Crop species
- Crop growth stage
- Soil type
- Root depth
- Temperature
- Solar radiation
- Wind
- Humidity
- Rainfall
- Groundwater availability
- Production system

There is no single irrigation schedule that is suitable for every crop or field.

## Crop Water Requirements

Crop water demand changes during the growing season.

Water requirements are affected by:
- Plant size
- Leaf area
- Growth stage
- Weather
- Root development
- Crop density

Water demand often increases as the crop canopy expands and may be particularly important during:
- Flowering
- Fruit set
- Fruit or grain filling
- Tuber development
- Other sensitive reproductive stages

Water demand may decrease as crops approach maturity, depending on the crop.

## Soil and Irrigation

Soil texture strongly affects irrigation frequency.

### Sandy Soil
- Drains rapidly.
- Holds less available water.
- Often requires smaller and more frequent irrigations.

### Clay Soil
- Holds more water.
- Drains more slowly.
- May require less frequent irrigation but can become waterlogged if excessive water is applied.

### Loamy Soil
- Often provides a balance between water retention and drainage.

Root depth is also important. Deep-rooted crops can access water from a larger soil volume than shallow-rooted crops.

## Irrigation Methods

### Drip Irrigation
Drip irrigation delivers water close to the plant root zone.

Advantages can include:
- Efficient water use.
- Reduced foliage wetness.
- Reduced evaporation from some surfaces.
- More precise water delivery.
- Suitability for many vegetable, fruit, and high-value crops.

Potential limitations include:
- Higher initial cost.
- Emitter clogging.
- Need for filtration and maintenance.
- Uneven application if the system is poorly designed.

### Sprinkler Irrigation
Sprinklers distribute water over the crop or soil surface.

Advantages include:
- Broad field coverage.
- Suitability for certain field and overhead cooling applications.
- Useful for crop establishment in some systems.

Potential disadvantages include:
- Greater evaporation.
- Increased foliage wetness.
- Increased risk of some foliar diseases.
- Wind-related distribution problems.

### Surface Irrigation
Water is distributed over the soil surface through methods such as:
- Furrows
- Basins
- Borders

Advantages can include lower equipment requirements.

Potential disadvantages include:
- Lower application efficiency in poorly designed systems.
- Uneven water distribution.
- Soil erosion.
- Waterlogging in poorly drained soils.

### Subsurface Irrigation
Water is delivered below the soil surface, often through buried drip systems.

It can reduce surface evaporation and keep foliage dry but generally requires careful planning, installation, and maintenance.

## Irrigation Timing

Irrigation should be based on crop water demand and root-zone moisture rather than a fixed calendar schedule alone.

Useful indicators include:
- Soil moisture.
- Crop growth stage.
- Recent rainfall.
- Weather forecast.
- Temperature.
- Evapotranspiration.
- Plant symptoms.
- Soil texture.

Over-irrigation should be avoided even when the crop appears healthy.

## Soil Moisture Monitoring

Soil moisture can be assessed using:
- Manual soil examination.
- Soil-moisture probes.
- Tensiometers.
- Capacitance sensors.
- Other field monitoring tools.

Monitoring should focus on the crop's effective root zone.

Frequent shallow irrigation may be appropriate for some shallow-rooted or newly established crops, while established deep-rooted crops may benefit from deeper and less frequent irrigation.

## Irrigation and Plant Symptoms

### Under-Irrigation
Possible symptoms include:
- Wilting.
- Leaf curling.
- Reduced leaf expansion.
- Reduced growth.
- Flower or fruit drop.
- Reduced fruit or grain size.

### Over-Irrigation
Possible symptoms include:
- Yellowing.
- Poor growth.
- Root damage.
- Increased root disease.
- Reduced oxygen in the root zone.
- Nutrient loss.

These symptoms can overlap with disease and nutrient disorders, so irrigation problems should not be diagnosed from plant appearance alone.

## Irrigation and Nutrient Management

Water availability strongly affects nutrient uptake.

Poor irrigation management can cause:
- Nutrient leaching.
- Salt accumulation.
- Reduced nutrient uptake.
- Root damage.

Excessive irrigation after fertilizer application can move mobile nutrients deeper into the soil beyond the main root zone.

Fertigation systems should be managed according to crop nutrient requirements and water delivery.

## Irrigation and Disease

Excessive leaf wetness can increase the risk of many foliar diseases.

Where practical:
- Prefer root-zone irrigation for disease-sensitive crops.
- Irrigate when foliage can dry quickly.
- Avoid unnecessary nighttime overhead irrigation.
- Improve canopy airflow.

Not all diseases respond the same way, so irrigation recommendations should be disease-specific when a pathogen is already identified.

## Irrigation Water Quality

Water quality can affect:
- Soil salinity.
- Soil sodicity.
- Nutrient availability.
- Crop tolerance.
- Irrigation system performance.

Water testing may be useful when there are concerns about:
- Electrical conductivity.
- Sodium.
- Chloride.
- Bicarbonate.
- Other quality parameters.

Poor-quality water can gradually degrade soil and reduce crop productivity.

## Irrigation During Rainfall

When significant rainfall occurs:
- Reassess soil moisture before irrigating.
- Avoid automatic irrigation based solely on the normal schedule.
- Consider rainfall amount and distribution.
- Check whether water reached the effective root zone.
- Monitor drainage and standing water.

Light rainfall may not provide enough water to replace the crop's full irrigation requirement.

## Irrigation During Hot Weather

Hot conditions can increase evapotranspiration and crop water demand.

During heat events:
- Monitor soil moisture more frequently.
- Check crops during the most stressful part of the day.
- Adjust irrigation according to actual root-zone conditions.
- Avoid severe drying of shallow-rooted crops.
- Avoid excessive irrigation that creates waterlogging.

Irrigation cannot completely eliminate heat stress when air temperature exceeds crop tolerance.

## Irrigation During Cold Weather

Cold conditions generally reduce evapotranspiration but may still require irrigation if soil moisture is low.

Avoid unnecessary irrigation when:
- Soil is already adequately moist.
- Drainage is poor.
- Cold, saturated conditions may increase root damage.

Frost-protection irrigation is a separate, crop-specific practice and should only be recommended using current local guidance.

## Irrigation Scheduling

A useful irrigation schedule should consider:
- Crop type.
- Crop growth stage.
- Root depth.
- Soil texture.
- Soil moisture.
- Rainfall.
- Weather forecast.
- Evapotranspiration.
- Irrigation-system efficiency.

Scheduling should be adjusted as crop and weather conditions change.

## Water-Use Efficiency

Water-use efficiency can be improved by:
- Irrigating based on crop demand.
- Maintaining appropriate soil organic matter.
- Using mulch where suitable.
- Reducing leaks.
- Maintaining irrigation equipment.
- Applying water uniformly.
- Using drip irrigation where economically appropriate.
- Avoiding runoff and excessive evaporation.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Assess water availability.
- Check irrigation-system capacity.
- Evaluate soil drainage.
- Determine soil texture.
- Plan irrigation method.
- Consider crop water requirements.
- Test irrigation water where quality concerns exist.

### During Growth
- Monitor soil moisture.
- Adjust irrigation according to crop growth stage.
- Consider recent rainfall.
- Monitor weather.
- Maintain irrigation equipment.
- Avoid excessive irrigation.
- Check for leaks and uneven distribution.
- Inspect crops for drought or waterlogging symptoms.

### After Harvest
- Review total irrigation use.
- Identify fields with drainage or distribution problems.
- Maintain irrigation equipment.
- Inspect pipes, filters, emitters, pumps, and valves.
- Record water-use information for future planning.

## Cultural Practices

Important practices include:
- Maintaining soil organic matter.
- Using mulch where appropriate.
- Improving soil structure.
- Maintaining drainage.
- Reducing runoff.
- Managing weeds.
- Using crop-appropriate irrigation systems.
- Monitoring soil moisture.
- Maintaining irrigation equipment.
- Adjusting irrigation after rainfall.

## Biological / Organic Management

No biological treatment is required for irrigation itself.

Soil-health practices can improve water management by supporting:
- Better infiltration.
- Better soil structure.
- Greater water-holding capacity.
- Reduced runoff.
- Improved root development.

Useful practices include:
- Cover crops.
- Organic matter management.
- Mulching.
- Reduced unnecessary soil disturbance.

## Chemical Management

Irrigation itself does not normally require chemical treatment.

However, water-treatment chemicals or nutrient products may be used in specialized irrigation systems, such as fertigation or irrigation-water sanitation systems.

These should only be used according to:
- System requirements.
- Water-quality results.
- Crop requirements.
- Product labels.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

Do not add fertilizers, pesticides, acids, or other chemicals to irrigation water without confirming compatibility, concentration, crop safety, and legal requirements.

## Immediate Actions

When irrigation problems are suspected:

1. Check soil moisture in the effective root zone.
2. Review recent rainfall.
3. Inspect the irrigation system for leaks or blockages.
4. Check whether water is being distributed uniformly.
5. Examine plants for drought or waterlogging symptoms.
6. Check soil drainage.
7. Review crop growth stage.
8. Adjust irrigation according to actual crop demand.
9. Avoid unnecessary irrigation when the soil is already sufficiently wet.
10. Consult a local irrigation or agricultural specialist for persistent problems.

## Prevention

- Monitor soil moisture.
- Adjust irrigation according to crop demand.
- Consider rainfall before irrigating.
- Maintain good drainage.
- Use efficient irrigation systems where appropriate.
- Maintain irrigation equipment.
- Avoid prolonged soil saturation.
- Avoid severe drought stress.
- Test water quality where necessary.
- Maintain healthy soil structure and organic matter.
- Record irrigation practices and crop responses.
