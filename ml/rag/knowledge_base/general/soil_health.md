# Soil Health

## Topic
Soil Health

## Overview

Soil health refers to the capacity of soil to function as a living system that supports plant growth, regulates water, cycles nutrients, supports beneficial organisms, and maintains environmental quality.

Healthy agricultural soil generally has:
- Good structure
- Adequate organic matter
- Suitable pH
- Adequate nutrient availability
- Good water infiltration
- Appropriate water-holding capacity
- Sufficient biological activity
- Low levels of harmful compaction or contamination

Soil health is influenced by:
- Crop management
- Tillage
- Irrigation
- Fertilization
- Organic matter inputs
- Crop rotation
- Erosion
- Weather
- Soil type

## Soil Texture

Soil texture refers to the relative proportions of:
- Sand
- Silt
- Clay

Texture affects:
- Water-holding capacity
- Drainage
- Nutrient retention
- Aeration
- Root growth
- Soil workability

### Sandy Soil
Sandy soils generally:
- Drain rapidly.
- Hold less water.
- Have lower nutrient-retention capacity than clay-rich soils.
- May require more frequent irrigation and nutrient management.

### Clay Soil
Clay-rich soils generally:
- Hold more water.
- Retain nutrients effectively.
- Drain more slowly.
- Can become compacted when worked while excessively wet.

### Loamy Soil
Loam contains a relatively balanced mixture of sand, silt, and clay and is often suitable for many crops when structure and organic matter are maintained.

## Soil Structure

Soil structure refers to how individual soil particles are arranged into aggregates.

Good structure generally supports:
- Root growth
- Water infiltration
- Drainage
- Air movement
- Microbial activity

Poor structure can result from:
- Excessive tillage
- Compaction
- Low organic matter
- Heavy traffic
- Working soil when excessively wet

## Soil Organic Matter

Organic matter contributes to:
- Soil aggregation
- Water-holding capacity
- Nutrient cycling
- Microbial activity
- Carbon storage
- Root-zone quality

Sources of organic matter can include:
- Crop residues
- Compost
- Well-managed manure
- Cover crops
- Green manure
- Other suitable organic amendments

Organic amendments should be applied according to crop and soil requirements because excessive applications can contribute to nutrient accumulation or environmental losses.

## Soil pH

Soil pH affects:
- Nutrient availability
- Microbial activity
- Root development
- Chemical reactions in soil

Very acidic or alkaline soils can reduce the availability of certain nutrients.

Potential problems associated with unsuitable pH include:
- Nutrient deficiencies
- Nutrient toxicity
- Reduced microbial activity
- Poor root growth

Soil pH should be measured through soil testing rather than estimated from plant symptoms alone.

## Soil Nutrients

Important plant nutrients include:
- Nitrogen
- Phosphorus
- Potassium
- Calcium
- Magnesium
- Sulfur
- Iron
- Zinc
- Manganese
- Copper
- Boron
- Molybdenum
- Chlorine
- Nickel

Nutrient availability depends not only on total nutrient content but also on:
- Soil pH
- Soil moisture
- Organic matter
- Microbial activity
- Root health
- Nutrient interactions

## Soil Testing

Soil testing can help determine:
- Soil pH
- Nutrient status
- Organic matter
- Salinity indicators
- Other soil properties depending on the test

A useful soil-test program should:
- Collect representative samples.
- Use appropriate sampling depth.
- Avoid sampling only visibly abnormal areas unless diagnosing a specific problem.
- Use a reliable laboratory.
- Interpret results using crop-specific recommendations.

Soil tests should ideally be performed before major fertilizer or amendment decisions.

## Soil Moisture

Suitable soil moisture is necessary for:
- Seed germination
- Root growth
- Nutrient uptake
- Photosynthesis
- Reproductive development

Both drought and excess water can damage crops.

### Too Little Moisture
Can cause:
- Wilting
- Reduced growth
- Nutrient-uptake problems
- Flower and fruit loss
- Reduced yield

### Too Much Moisture
Can cause:
- Reduced root-zone oxygen
- Root damage
- Nutrient loss
- Increased root-disease risk
- Poor plant growth

## Soil Compaction

Compaction occurs when soil particles are compressed and pore space is reduced.

Possible effects include:
- Restricted root growth
- Reduced infiltration
- Poor drainage
- Reduced aeration
- Increased runoff
- Reduced nutrient uptake

Common causes include:
- Heavy machinery
- Repeated traffic
- Working wet soil
- High animal or equipment pressure

Management can include:
- Controlled traffic
- Avoiding field operations on excessively wet soil
- Maintaining organic matter
- Appropriate tillage where required
- Deep-rooted crops and cover crops where suitable

## Erosion

Soil erosion can remove:
- Topsoil
- Organic matter
- Nutrients
- Soil structure

Erosion can occur through:
- Wind
- Water
- Intensive surface runoff

Prevention measures can include:
- Vegetative cover
- Mulching
- Cover crops
- Contour farming where appropriate
- Reduced soil disturbance
- Terracing where appropriate
- Maintaining crop residues

## Salinity and Sodicity

Excess soluble salts can interfere with plant water uptake and cause:
- Poor germination
- Leaf burn
- Reduced growth
- Wilting despite moist soil

Sodic soils can also have poor structure and infiltration.

Potential causes include:
- Saline irrigation water
- Poor drainage
- High evaporation
- Inappropriate irrigation management

Diagnosis should involve appropriate soil or water testing.

## Soil Biological Activity

Soil contains:
- Bacteria
- Fungi
- Protozoa
- Nematodes
- Arthropods
- Earthworms
- Other organisms

Beneficial soil organisms contribute to:
- Nutrient cycling
- Organic matter decomposition
- Soil aggregation
- Root interactions
- Suppression of some soil-borne pathogens

Management practices that can support beneficial biological activity include:
- Maintaining organic matter
- Reducing unnecessary soil disturbance
- Maintaining crop diversity
- Using cover crops
- Avoiding unnecessary pesticide applications

## Soil and Root Health

Root health depends on:
- Adequate oxygen
- Suitable moisture
- Appropriate soil structure
- Nutrient availability
- Suitable pH
- Low levels of harmful salts
- Low pathogen pressure

Poor root health can produce above-ground symptoms that resemble:
- Nutrient deficiency
- Drought stress
- Disease
- Chemical injury

When plants show unexplained decline, the root zone should be examined rather than focusing only on leaves.

## Crop Rotation

Crop rotation can improve soil health by:
- Increasing crop diversity
- Reducing some soil-borne pest and disease pressure
- Supporting different rooting patterns
- Improving nutrient cycling
- Reducing repeated depletion of particular nutrients

Rotations should be designed according to:
- Local climate
- Soil type
- Crop requirements
- Major pests and diseases
- Farm economics

## Cover Crops

Cover crops can:
- Protect soil from erosion.
- Add organic matter.
- Improve soil structure.
- Reduce weed pressure.
- Capture nutrients.
- Improve soil biological activity.

Cover crops should be selected based on:
- Local climate
- Soil type
- Water availability
- Crop rotation
- Termination method
- Pest and disease considerations

## Soil Health and Fertilization

Fertilizer management should be based on:
- Soil-test results
- Crop nutrient requirements
- Expected yield
- Existing nutrient availability

Excessive fertilizer application can:
- Increase nutrient losses.
- Increase salinity risk.
- Contribute to water pollution.
- Cause nutrient imbalance.

Soil health is better supported by balanced nutrient management than by maximizing fertilizer application.

## Soil Health and Irrigation

Good irrigation management should:
- Supply sufficient water to the root zone.
- Avoid prolonged saturation.
- Minimize erosion.
- Reduce nutrient leaching.
- Match water application to crop demand.

Improving soil organic matter and structure can improve water retention and infiltration.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Test soil before major nutrient or amendment decisions.
- Measure soil pH.
- Assess drainage.
- Evaluate compaction.
- Review previous crop history.
- Plan crop rotation.
- Consider cover crops where appropriate.
- Apply amendments according to soil-test recommendations.

### During Growth
- Monitor soil moisture.
- Avoid unnecessary soil compaction.
- Maintain surface cover where appropriate.
- Manage irrigation carefully.
- Monitor plant growth for nutrient or root-zone problems.
- Maintain balanced fertilization.
- Reduce erosion.
- Maintain suitable organic matter inputs.

### After Harvest
- Manage crop residues appropriately.
- Maintain soil cover where suitable.
- Avoid unnecessary traffic on wet fields.
- Review soil-test requirements.
- Plan crop rotation and cover crops.
- Record soil-related problems for future seasons.

## Cultural Practices

Important practices include:
- Regular soil testing.
- Maintaining organic matter.
- Crop rotation.
- Cover cropping.
- Appropriate residue management.
- Avoiding excessive tillage.
- Avoiding traffic on wet soil.
- Maintaining suitable drainage.
- Preventing erosion.
- Balanced fertilization.
- Efficient irrigation.

## Immediate Actions

When soil-related crop problems are suspected:

1. Check recent irrigation and rainfall.
2. Inspect drainage and standing-water areas.
3. Examine roots where practical.
4. Assess whether the soil is compacted.
5. Review fertilizer history.
6. Conduct a soil test where appropriate.
7. Check soil pH.
8. Consider salinity or sodicity where symptoms and location suggest a risk.
9. Compare affected and unaffected field areas.
10. Consult a soil or crop-management expert when the cause is unclear.

## Prevention

- Test soil periodically.
- Maintain organic matter.
- Use crop rotation.
- Use cover crops where appropriate.
- Prevent erosion.
- Avoid unnecessary soil disturbance.
- Avoid field traffic on excessively wet soil.
- Maintain suitable drainage.
- Apply nutrients according to soil-test and crop requirements.
- Manage irrigation according to soil and crop needs.
- Monitor soil-related problems over multiple seasons.
