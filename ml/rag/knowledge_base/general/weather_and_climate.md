# Weather and Climate

## Topic
Weather and Climate

## Overview

Weather and climate strongly influence crop growth, water requirements, pest development, disease pressure, flowering, pollination, maturity, and yield.

Weather refers to short-term atmospheric conditions such as:
- Temperature
- Rainfall
- Humidity
- Wind
- Solar radiation
- Cloud cover

Climate refers to longer-term patterns of these conditions in a location.

Agricultural decisions should consider both current weather and the normal climate of the production area.

## Temperature

Temperature affects:
- Seed germination
- Root growth
- Photosynthesis
- Flowering
- Pollination
- Fruit development
- Grain filling
- Pest development
- Disease development
- Crop maturity

Each crop has a suitable temperature range.

### Heat Stress

Excessively high temperatures can cause:
- Leaf wilting
- Reduced photosynthesis
- Increased water demand
- Flower abortion
- Poor pollination
- Reduced fruit set
- Sunscald
- Reduced grain or fruit development

Heat stress can become more severe when high temperature occurs together with:
- Low soil moisture
- Low humidity
- Strong winds
- High solar radiation

### Cold Stress

Low temperatures can cause:
- Slow germination
- Reduced growth
- Poor nutrient uptake
- Delayed flowering
- Poor pollination
- Chilling injury
- Frost injury

Some crops are particularly sensitive during flowering and early fruit development.

## Rainfall

Rainfall provides water needed for crop growth but excessive rainfall can also create problems.

Adequate rainfall can:
- Replenish soil moisture.
- Support nutrient transport.
- Reduce drought stress.

Excessive rainfall can:
- Cause waterlogging.
- Reduce soil oxygen.
- Damage roots.
- Increase nutrient leaching.
- Increase fungal and bacterial disease pressure.
- Delay field operations.
- Increase soil erosion.

Rainfall immediately before harvest can also influence fruit cracking, disease development, and harvest quality in susceptible crops.

## Soil Moisture and Weather

Crop water requirements depend on:
- Crop species
- Growth stage
- Temperature
- Solar radiation
- Wind
- Humidity
- Soil type
- Root depth
- Existing soil moisture

High temperatures and strong winds generally increase water loss through evapotranspiration.

Weather-based irrigation decisions should therefore not rely on rainfall alone.

## Humidity

Relative humidity affects:
- Plant water loss
- Disease development
- Pest development
- Fungal spore production
- Drying of foliage

High humidity can favor many fungal and bacterial diseases, particularly when combined with prolonged leaf wetness.

Low humidity can increase:
- Plant water loss
- Drought stress
- Spider mite development in some production systems

Humidity should therefore be considered together with temperature and leaf wetness rather than used alone to predict disease risk.

## Leaf Wetness

Leaf wetness refers to the presence of water on plant surfaces caused by:
- Rain
- Dew
- Fog
- Irrigation
- Condensation

Leaf wetness duration can be an important factor in disease development.

Some fungal and bacterial pathogens require a sufficient period of wetness before infection can occur.

Reducing unnecessary leaf wetness can therefore help lower disease risk for susceptible crops.

## Wind

Wind affects:
- Evapotranspiration
- Plant water loss
- Pollination
- Physical plant damage
- Disease-spore movement
- Insect movement
- Spray drift

Strong winds can:
- Increase water loss.
- Break branches or stems.
- Damage leaves.
- Cause fruit rubbing.
- Spread airborne pathogens.

Wind can also transport fungal spores and insect vectors across fields.

## Solar Radiation

Solar radiation provides the energy required for photosynthesis.

Insufficient sunlight can:
- Reduce photosynthesis.
- Slow growth.
- Reduce carbohydrate production.
- Affect flowering and fruit development.

Excessive radiation combined with high temperatures can contribute to:
- Heat stress
- Sunscald
- Leaf damage
- Fruit damage

Canopy management can help balance light interception and heat exposure.

## Drought

Drought occurs when available water is insufficient to meet crop demand.

Drought stress can cause:
- Wilting
- Leaf curling
- Reduced leaf expansion
- Reduced photosynthesis
- Flower abortion
- Poor fruit set
- Reduced yield
- Smaller fruit or grain
- Premature leaf senescence

Drought can also make plants more vulnerable to some pests and diseases.

## Waterlogging

Waterlogging occurs when excess water fills soil pore spaces and reduces oxygen availability around roots.

Waterlogging can cause:
- Root suffocation
- Root decay
- Nutrient-uptake problems
- Leaf yellowing
- Reduced plant growth
- Increased susceptibility to root diseases

Improving drainage is generally more effective than repeatedly adding fertilizer to plants suffering from waterlogged roots.

## Frost

Frost can damage susceptible crops when tissue temperatures fall below critical levels.

Frost injury can cause:
- Blackened or water-soaked tissue
- Wilting after temperature recovery
- Damaged flowers
- Fruit injury
- Shoot death
- Crop loss

Sensitivity varies by:
- Crop
- Variety
- Growth stage
- Duration of cold exposure
- Minimum temperature

Young seedlings and flowering plants can be particularly vulnerable.

## Hail

Hail can physically damage:
- Leaves
- Stems
- Flowers
- Fruit

Hail injury may create wounds that later become entry points for pathogens.

After hail:
- Inspect damaged tissues.
- Remove severely damaged material where practical.
- Monitor wounds for secondary infection.
- Maintain appropriate plant nutrition and irrigation.

## Extreme Weather and Disease

Weather can significantly influence disease risk.

Disease development often increases when conditions provide:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Frequent rainfall
- Dense canopies

However, the exact conditions required differ among pathogens.

A farmer should therefore avoid assuming that every disease becomes more likely simply because weather is humid or rainy.

## Weather and Pest Development

Temperature strongly influences the development rate of many insects and mites.

Warm conditions can accelerate:
- Egg development
- Larval development
- Reproduction
- Population growth

Some pests increase rapidly during hot and dry weather, while others favor cooler or wetter conditions.

Integrated pest management should therefore use crop-specific pest monitoring together with weather conditions.

## Weather and Irrigation

Irrigation decisions should consider:
- Recent rainfall.
- Forecast rainfall.
- Soil moisture.
- Crop growth stage.
- Temperature.
- Wind.
- Humidity.
- Soil type.

Avoid irrigation when soil moisture is already sufficient.

Avoid creating prolonged saturated soil conditions.

Where possible:
- Irrigate near the root zone.
- Apply water according to crop demand.
- Use soil-moisture information where available.
- Adjust irrigation after major rainfall events.

## Weather Forecast Use

Weather forecasts can support:
- Irrigation planning
- Disease-risk assessment
- Pest monitoring
- Frost preparation
- Harvest planning
- Spray timing
- Field-operation planning

Forecasts should not be treated as perfectly accurate.

Agricultural decisions should consider:
- Forecast confidence.
- Local field conditions.
- Crop growth stage.
- Soil moisture.
- Current disease or pest observations.

## Disease Prevention Based on Weather

When weather is forecast to favor a known crop disease:

1. Increase field scouting.
2. Inspect susceptible tissues.
3. Avoid unnecessary foliage wetting.
4. Improve canopy airflow where possible.
5. Review current local disease-warning systems.
6. Follow crop-specific preventive recommendations.
7. Avoid unnecessary pesticide application without confirming disease risk.

Weather information should support—not replace—field diagnosis and monitoring.

## Crop Monitoring During Extreme Weather

After extreme weather events, inspect for:
- Wilting
- Leaf scorch
- Frost injury
- Hail damage
- Flooding
- Root damage
- Sunscald
- Wind damage
- Disease development following wounds

Document the affected areas and compare them with unaffected portions of the field.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select crops and cultivars suitable for the local climate.
- Consider expected temperature extremes.
- Plan irrigation and drainage.
- Consider frost and heat risk.
- Use planting dates appropriate to local conditions.
- Select suitable field locations.
- Consider wind exposure and water availability.

### During Growth
- Monitor weather conditions regularly.
- Monitor soil moisture.
- Adjust irrigation according to crop demand.
- Watch for heat, drought, frost, flooding, and wind stress.
- Increase pest and disease scouting during favorable weather periods.
- Protect susceptible crops where practical.
- Maintain appropriate drainage and canopy management.

### After Harvest
- Review weather-related crop losses.
- Record disease and pest outbreaks associated with specific weather conditions.
- Review irrigation performance.
- Assess drainage problems.
- Use observations to improve planning for the next season.

## Immediate Actions

When unusual weather is expected:

1. Check the current crop growth stage.
2. Assess soil moisture.
3. Review the expected temperature and rainfall.
4. Check for heat, frost, wind, or flooding risk.
5. Adjust irrigation when necessary.
6. Increase scouting if weather favors known pests or diseases.
7. Protect sensitive crops where practical.
8. Inspect plants after the weather event.
9. Record damage and affected areas.
10. Consult a local agricultural expert for crop-specific recommendations when severe conditions occur.

## Prevention

- Select climate-adapted crops and cultivars.
- Use appropriate planting dates.
- Maintain suitable irrigation systems.
- Maintain effective drainage.
- Monitor soil moisture.
- Monitor weather forecasts.
- Use crop-specific disease and pest alerts where available.
- Maintain healthy plant growth.
- Protect vulnerable crops from extreme weather where practical.
- Keep records of weather-related problems.
- Adjust future crop-management decisions based on field history.
