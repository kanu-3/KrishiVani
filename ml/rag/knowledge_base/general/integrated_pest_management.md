# Integrated Pest Management

## Topic
Integrated Pest Management

## Overview

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a decision-making approach that combines multiple compatible methods to prevent and manage agricultural pests while reducing unnecessary pesticide use.

IPM can be used for:
- Insect pests
- Mites
- Nematodes
- Diseases
- Weeds
- Other crop pests

The main principles of IPM are:
- Prevention
- Monitoring
- Correct identification
- Decision-making based on pest risk
- Use of multiple compatible control methods
- Protection of beneficial organisms
- Responsible pesticide use

IPM does not mean avoiding pesticides completely. It means using pesticides when justified and integrating them with other management methods.

## Core Components of IPM

An effective IPM program generally includes:

1. Prevention
2. Monitoring
3. Identification
4. Assessment of pest risk
5. Selection of management methods
6. Evaluation of results
7. Record keeping

## Prevention

Preventive practices aim to keep pest populations below damaging levels.

Examples include:
- Using healthy planting material.
- Selecting resistant or tolerant varieties.
- Crop rotation.
- Weed management.
- Field sanitation.
- Appropriate planting dates.
- Suitable plant spacing.
- Maintaining crop vigor.
- Managing irrigation and nutrition.
- Excluding pests where practical.

Preventing establishment is often more efficient than controlling a severe infestation later.

## Correct Identification

Accurate identification is essential.

Before taking action, determine:
- What organism is present.
- What part of the crop is affected.
- Whether the organism is actually causing damage.
- Whether symptoms could have another cause.

Similar symptoms can result from:
- Pests
- Diseases
- Nutrient deficiencies
- Drought
- Waterlogging
- Herbicide injury
- Mechanical damage

A photograph alone may not always be sufficient for definitive identification.

## Monitoring

Regular monitoring helps detect pest populations early.

Monitoring methods can include:
- Visual scouting
- Sticky traps
- Pheromone traps
- Sweep nets
- Plant shaking
- Leaf inspection
- Soil sampling
- Root inspection
- Disease scouting
- Weed surveys

Monitoring should be systematic rather than limited to areas that already look damaged.

## Scouting Strategy

A useful scouting program should define:
- Fields to inspect.
- Sampling locations.
- Sampling frequency.
- Plant parts to examine.
- Pest stages to record.
- Disease symptoms to record.
- Weather conditions.

Field scouting should cover:
- Field edges.
- Known hotspots.
- New crop growth.
- Flowers.
- Fruit.
- Leaf undersides.
- Root zones where appropriate.

## Economic Thresholds

IPM does not require eliminating every pest.

An economic threshold is the pest level at which action should be taken to prevent the pest from reaching a level that causes economically unacceptable damage.

Thresholds depend on:
- Crop.
- Pest species.
- Crop growth stage.
- Crop value.
- Expected yield.
- Control cost.
- Environmental conditions.

Thresholds should come from current crop- and region-specific recommendations.

## Biological Control

Biological control uses natural enemies or biological agents to suppress pests.

Examples include:
- Predatory insects
- Predatory mites
- Parasitoid insects
- Entomopathogenic fungi
- Entomopathogenic bacteria
- Other biological control organisms

Common beneficial organisms include:
- Lady beetles
- Lacewings
- Predatory mites
- Parasitoid wasps

Biological control is most effective when natural enemies are protected and introduced or conserved at appropriate times.

## Cultural Control

Cultural controls modify crop management to reduce pest pressure.

Examples include:
- Crop rotation.
- Adjusting planting dates.
- Using resistant varieties.
- Appropriate plant spacing.
- Weed management.
- Residue management.
- Balanced nutrition.
- Irrigation management.
- Removal of infested plant material.

## Mechanical and Physical Control

Methods include:
- Hand removal.
- Pruning.
- Traps.
- Barriers.
- Row covers.
- Insect-proof mesh.
- Mulches.
- Mechanical cultivation.
- Removal of heavily infested plants.

These methods can be especially useful in:
- Small farms
- Nurseries
- Greenhouses
- High-value crops
- Early pest outbreaks

## Chemical Control

Pesticides may be used when:
- Pest populations exceed relevant thresholds.
- Crop damage is economically significant.
- Other methods are insufficient.
- The product is legally registered for the crop and pest.

Before application:
- Confirm the target pest.
- Confirm the crop.
- Check growth stage.
- Read the current product label.
- Follow application instructions.
- Check pre-harvest and re-entry requirements.
- Use appropriate protective equipment.

## Pesticide Selection

Pesticide selection should consider:
- Target pest.
- Pest life stage.
- Crop.
- Crop growth stage.
- Mode of action.
- Environmental conditions.
- Beneficial-organism sensitivity.
- Current local registration.

Do not choose pesticides simply because visible plant damage is severe.

Severe damage may remain visible after the pest population has already declined.

## Resistance Management

Repeated use of the same pesticide mode of action can select for resistant pest populations.

Resistance-management practices include:
- Rotating modes of action.
- Integrating nonchemical controls.
- Avoiding unnecessary treatments.
- Following labeled rates.
- Monitoring treatment effectiveness.
- Avoiding repeated back-to-back use of the same mode of action.

Increasing the pesticide dose above the labeled recommendation is not an appropriate response to suspected resistance.

## IPM and Beneficial Organisms

Beneficial organisms can help suppress pests naturally.

IPM programs should therefore:
- Monitor beneficial organisms.
- Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum pesticide use.
- Prefer selective products where practical.
- Avoid spraying when pest populations are below action thresholds.
- Protect habitats that support beneficial organisms where feasible.

## IPM and Weather

Weather influences:
- Pest reproduction.
- Disease development.
- Pest movement.
- Natural-enemy activity.
- Pesticide performance.
- Crop susceptibility.

IPM decisions should combine:
- Current pest observations.
- Crop growth stage.
- Weather conditions.
- Disease or pest forecasts where available.

Weather should not replace field monitoring.

## Disease IPM

IPM principles can also be applied to plant diseases.

Disease-management decisions may include:
- Resistant cultivars.
- Clean seed.
- Crop rotation.
- Sanitation.
- Canopy management.
- Irrigation management.
- Disease forecasting.
- Biological controls.
- Preventive fungicides or bactericides when justified.

## Weed IPM

Weed management can also follow IPM principles.

Methods include:
- Crop rotation.
- Cover crops.
- Mulching.
- Mechanical control.
- Timely cultivation.
- Competitive crop stands.
- Herbicides where required.

Correct weed identification and monitoring are important before treatment.

## Record Keeping

Useful records include:
- Pest species.
- Pest population.
- Crop growth stage.
- Weather conditions.
- Management actions.
- Product used.
- Application date.
- Treatment effectiveness.
- Natural-enemy observations.
- Yield impact.

Records help improve decisions in future seasons.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Review previous pest and disease problems.
- Select resistant or tolerant varieties.
- Use healthy seed and planting material.
- Plan crop rotation.
- Plan monitoring methods.
- Identify potential pest hotspots.
- Prepare sanitation and exclusion measures.

### During Growth
- Scout crops regularly.
- Identify pests accurately.
- Monitor natural enemies.
- Compare pest populations with relevant thresholds.
- Apply cultural and biological controls first where practical.
- Use physical controls where appropriate.
- Apply pesticides only when justified.
- Rotate pesticide modes of action.
- Record management results.

### After Harvest
- Remove or manage infested residues where appropriate.
- Control volunteer crops.
- Clean equipment.
- Review pest outbreaks.
- Record successful and unsuccessful controls.
- Plan improvements for the next crop cycle.

## Immediate Actions

When a pest or disease problem is detected:

1. Identify the affected crop and growth stage.
2. Determine the likely pest or pathogen.
3. Inspect surrounding plants.
4. Estimate the extent of the problem.
5. Check recent weather conditions.
6. Look for natural enemies.
7. Review relevant economic or action thresholds.
8. Select the least disruptive effective management method.
9. Monitor the result after intervention.
10. Escalate to chemical control only when justified and legally permitted.

## Prevention

- Start with healthy planting material.
- Use resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Practice crop rotation.
- Maintain field sanitation.
- Control weeds.
- Monitor crops regularly.
- Conserve beneficial organisms.
- Maintain crop vigor.
- Use physical controls where appropriate.
- Base pesticide applications on monitoring and thresholds.
- Rotate pesticide modes of action.
- Keep detailed records.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide regulations.
