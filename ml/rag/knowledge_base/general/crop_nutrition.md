# Crop Nutrition

## Topic
Crop Nutrition

## Overview

Crop nutrition refers to the availability, uptake, transport, and use of essential nutrients required for normal plant growth and development.

Plants require both macronutrients and micronutrients. Nutrient requirements vary by crop, cultivar, growth stage, soil type, climate, and expected yield.

Poor crop nutrition can result from:
- Nutrient deficiency
- Nutrient excess or toxicity
- Incorrect soil pH
- Poor root development
- Waterlogging
- Drought stress
- Nutrient interactions
- Poor nutrient availability in the soil

Visual symptoms can help identify possible nutritional problems, but symptoms can overlap with diseases, pests, and environmental stress. Soil or plant tissue testing may be required for confirmation.

## Essential Nutrients

### Macronutrients

Plants require relatively large quantities of:
- Nitrogen (N)
- Phosphorus (P)
- Potassium (K)
- Calcium (Ca)
- Magnesium (Mg)
- Sulfur (S)

### Micronutrients

Plants require smaller quantities of:
- Iron (Fe)
- Manganese (Mn)
- Zinc (Zn)
- Copper (Cu)
- Boron (B)
- Molybdenum (Mo)
- Chlorine (Cl)
- Nickel (Ni)

The required amount of a nutrient does not indicate its importance. Micronutrients are needed in smaller quantities but are still essential for normal plant development.

## Nitrogen

Nitrogen is important for:
- Leaf and shoot growth
- Chlorophyll production
- Photosynthesis
- Protein synthesis
- Overall vegetative development

### Possible Nitrogen Deficiency Symptoms
- Older leaves become pale green or yellow.
- Yellowing generally begins on older leaves because nitrogen is mobile within the plant.
- Plant growth may become slow or stunted.
- Leaves may be smaller than normal.
- Overall plant vigor may decline.

### Excess Nitrogen
Excess nitrogen can:
- Produce excessive vegetative growth.
- Delay flowering or maturity in some crops.
- Produce soft, succulent growth.
- Increase susceptibility to some pests and diseases.
- Reduce fruit or reproductive development in some production systems.

## Phosphorus

Phosphorus contributes to:
- Root development
- Energy transfer
- Flowering
- Seed formation
- Early plant establishment

### Possible Phosphorus Deficiency Symptoms
- Slow growth.
- Poor root development.
- Older leaves may develop dark green, bluish, purple, or reddish coloration depending on crop and conditions.
- Delayed maturity may occur.
- Reproductive development can be reduced.

Phosphorus availability is strongly influenced by soil chemistry and pH.

## Potassium

Potassium is important for:
- Water regulation
- Stomatal function
- Enzyme activity
- Stress tolerance
- Carbohydrate transport
- Fruit and grain development

### Possible Potassium Deficiency Symptoms
- Older leaves may develop yellowing along margins.
- Leaf margins may become brown or scorched.
- Necrosis can progress inward from leaf edges.
- Plants may have weak stems.
- Fruit or grain development may be reduced.

Potassium deficiency symptoms can resemble drought or salinity stress.

## Calcium

Calcium contributes to:
- Cell-wall development
- Cell membrane stability
- Root and shoot growth
- Normal development of rapidly growing tissues

### Possible Calcium Deficiency Symptoms
- Symptoms usually appear first on young tissues.
- Growing points may become distorted or die.
- Young leaves may develop abnormal shapes.
- Root growth may be impaired.
- Some fruits may develop physiological disorders associated with inadequate calcium supply.

Calcium uptake depends strongly on adequate root-zone moisture and healthy root function.

## Magnesium

Magnesium is a central component of chlorophyll and is important for:
- Photosynthesis
- Enzyme activity
- Energy metabolism

### Possible Magnesium Deficiency Symptoms
- Older leaves may develop interveinal chlorosis.
- Veins may remain green while tissue between veins becomes yellow.
- Severe deficiency can progress to necrosis.

## Sulfur

Sulfur is involved in:
- Protein synthesis
- Enzyme activity
- Formation of sulfur-containing compounds

### Possible Sulfur Deficiency Symptoms
- Young leaves may become pale or yellow.
- Growth may become reduced.
- Plants can develop a generally pale appearance.

Unlike nitrogen deficiency, sulfur deficiency often becomes more noticeable on younger tissues.

## Iron

Iron is important for:
- Chlorophyll formation
- Photosynthesis
- Electron transport

### Possible Iron Deficiency Symptoms
- Young leaves develop interveinal chlorosis.
- Veins may remain green while surrounding tissue becomes yellow.
- Severe deficiency can cause nearly complete yellowing of young leaves.

Iron availability is strongly influenced by soil pH and soil chemistry.

## Zinc

Zinc contributes to:
- Enzyme activity
- Hormone regulation
- Growth and development

### Possible Zinc Deficiency Symptoms
- Shortened internodes.
- Small leaves.
- Chlorosis.
- Reduced plant growth.

## Manganese

Manganese is important for:
- Photosynthesis
- Enzyme activity
- Metabolic processes

### Possible Manganese Deficiency Symptoms
- Interveinal chlorosis.
- Small necrotic spots in severe cases.
- Reduced growth.

## Boron

Boron contributes to:
- Cell-wall formation
- Reproductive development
- Pollen growth
- Meristem development

### Possible Boron Deficiency Symptoms
- Death or deformation of growing points.
- Poor flowering or fruit set.
- Abnormal young leaves.
- Cracking or deformities in some crops.

Both deficiency and excess boron can damage plants, so applications should be based on reliable soil or plant information.

## Nutrient Mobility and Symptom Location

Nutrient mobility within the plant can influence where deficiency symptoms first appear.

### Mobile Nutrients

Deficiencies of mobile nutrients often appear first on older leaves.

Examples include:
- Nitrogen
- Phosphorus
- Potassium
- Magnesium

### Less Mobile Nutrients

Deficiencies of less-mobile nutrients often appear first on young leaves or growing points.

Examples include:
- Calcium
- Boron
- Iron

This distinction is useful for preliminary diagnosis but should not be treated as definitive because environmental conditions and crop species can change symptom expression.

## Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH strongly affects nutrient availability.

Very acidic or alkaline soils can reduce the availability of particular nutrients even when the total nutrient quantity in the soil is adequate.

Incorrect pH can contribute to:
- Iron deficiency
- Zinc deficiency
- Phosphorus availability problems
- Manganese toxicity or deficiency
- Calcium and magnesium availability problems

Soil testing should therefore be considered before making major corrective fertilizer applications.

## Nutrient Interactions

Nutrients interact with each other.

Excessive application of one nutrient can interfere with uptake or utilization of another.

Examples include:
- Excess phosphorus affecting zinc availability.
- Excess potassium interfering with magnesium uptake.
- Excess calcium affecting availability of some micronutrients.
- Excess nitrogen producing nutrient imbalance and excessive vegetative growth.

For this reason, applying a single nutrient without understanding the overall nutrient status can worsen crop problems.

## Diagnosis

Nutrient problems can resemble:
- Fungal diseases
- Bacterial diseases
- Viral diseases
- Insect damage
- Herbicide injury
- Drought stress
- Waterlogging
- Salinity

Possible nutrient deficiency should therefore be evaluated using:
- Symptom location on the plant
- Crop growth stage
- Soil characteristics
- Recent fertilizer history
- Irrigation history
- Weather conditions
- Soil testing
- Plant tissue testing where appropriate

Visual diagnosis alone should not be used as the sole basis for large fertilizer applications.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Conduct soil testing where practical.
- Determine soil pH.
- Assess existing nutrient levels.
- Select crops and cultivars suitable for the soil and production system.
- Incorporate soil amendments according to test recommendations.
- Maintain suitable organic matter.

### During Growth
- Monitor plants regularly for nutrient-related symptoms.
- Use soil and plant analysis where available.
- Apply nutrients according to crop requirements and soil-test results.
- Split nutrient applications when appropriate for the crop.
- Maintain proper irrigation to support nutrient uptake.
- Avoid excessive fertilizer application.
- Correct severe nutrient imbalances using locally recommended practices.

### After Harvest
- Review crop yield and nutrient-removal information.
- Reassess soil nutrient status when appropriate.
- Adjust the following season's nutrient-management plan.
- Maintain or improve soil organic matter.
- Use crop residues appropriately according to the production system.

## Fertilizer Management

Fertilizer applications should be based on:
- Crop nutrient requirements.
- Soil-test results.
- Expected yield.
- Crop growth stage.
- Nutrient availability.
- Previous fertilizer applications.
- Irrigation practices.
- Local recommendations.

Avoid applying fertilizer solely because a leaf appears yellow.

Yellowing can result from:
- Nitrogen deficiency
- Iron deficiency
- Waterlogging
- Root damage
- Disease
- Pest feeding
- Drought
- Herbicide injury

## Organic Matter

Organic matter can improve:
- Soil structure
- Water-holding capacity
- Nutrient cycling
- Microbial activity
- Root development

Sources can include:
- Well-managed crop residues
- Compost
- Properly treated organic amendments
- Cover crops
- Green manure

Organic amendments should be used carefully because their nutrient content varies and excessive applications can contribute to nutrient accumulation or environmental losses.

## Immediate Actions

When a nutrient problem is suspected:

1. Identify which plant parts show symptoms.
2. Determine whether symptoms occur mainly on old or young leaves.
3. Check irrigation and drainage conditions.
4. Review recent fertilizer applications.
5. Consider recent weather conditions.
6. Inspect roots where practical for signs of waterlogging or root damage.
7. Conduct a soil test where appropriate.
8. Consider plant tissue testing for difficult cases.
9. Avoid applying large quantities of a nutrient before confirming the likely cause.
10. Consult a local agricultural expert for crop-specific correction recommendations.

## Prevention

- Test soil periodically.
- Maintain appropriate soil pH.
- Apply nutrients according to crop and soil requirements.
- Avoid excessive fertilization.
- Maintain healthy roots through appropriate irrigation and drainage.
- Maintain adequate organic matter.
- Monitor plant growth throughout the season.
- Keep fertilizer records.
- Use crop-specific nutrient recommendations.
- Reassess nutrient requirements after major yield or management changes.
