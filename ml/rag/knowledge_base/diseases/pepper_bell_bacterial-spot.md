# Pepper, Bell Bacterial Spot

## Crop
Pepper, Bell

## Crop Aliases
- Bell pepper
- Sweet pepper
- Pepper
- Capsicum
- *Capsicum annuum*

## Disease
Bacterial Spot

## Disease Aliases
- Pepper bacterial spot
- Bacterial spot of pepper
- Bacterial leaf spot
- Bacterial spot of bell pepper

## Scientific Name
*Xanthomonas* species complex

## Common Names
- Bacterial spot
- Bacterial spot of pepper
- Bacterial leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The disease affects:
- Bell pepper
- Hot pepper
- Other *Capsicum* species
- Tomato and some other solanaceous crops can also be affected by related *Xanthomonas* strains

## Symptoms

Bacterial spot can affect leaves, stems, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small water-soaked spots may initially appear on young leaves.
- Lesions become dark green, brown, or black as infected tissue dies.
- Spots may enlarge and merge under favorable conditions.
- Leaf lesions can become irregular or angular.
- Severely infected leaves may yellow and drop prematurely.
- Older lesions may become dry and brittle.

### Stems
- Small dark lesions may develop on stems and petioles.
- Severe infection can weaken affected tissues.
- Stem lesions can contribute to premature leaf loss or reduced plant vigor.

### Fruit
- Small water-soaked spots may develop on immature fruit.
- Lesions become raised, rough, brown, or dark as infection progresses.
- Fruit spots can become scabby and corky.
- Multiple lesions may occur on individual fruits.
- Severe infection reduces fruit quality and marketability.

## Disease Development and Spread

The pathogen can survive in infected seed, crop residue, volunteer plants, and other infected plant material depending on the pathogen population and production system.

Spread occurs primarily through:
- Rain splash
- Overhead irrigation
- Wind-driven rain
- Handling of wet plants
- Contaminated tools
- Movement of infected seedlings
- Infected seed

The bacteria enter tissues through natural openings or wounds.

Once established, repeated wetting events can lead to new infection cycles and rapid disease spread through a crop.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Overhead irrigation
- Dense plantings
- Wet foliage during field operations
- Presence of infected plant material

Disease can spread rapidly when plants remain wet for extended periods.

## Detection

Pepper bacterial spot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop small dark water-soaked spots.
- Leaf lesions enlarge and merge.
- Lesions become brown or black.
- Fruit develops raised, rough, corky spots.
- Disease increases following repeated rainfall or overhead irrigation.
- Similar symptoms occur across multiple plants in a wet field.

Because several bacterial and fungal diseases can produce leaf spots, visual symptoms should be interpreted together with crop history and environmental conditions.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Bacterial spot can cause:
- Leaf spotting
- Premature leaf drop
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Stem lesions
- Fruit spotting and scarring
- Reduced fruit marketability
- Reduced yield under severe disease pressure

Severe early-season infections can substantially weaken young plants.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use certified disease-free seed where available.
- Obtain healthy transplants from reputable nurseries.
- Avoid planting seedlings with visible leaf or stem lesions.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Avoid planting peppers immediately after fields with severe bacterial spot where practical.
- Maintain suitable spacing to improve airflow.

### During Growth
- Scout plants regularly for leaf and fruit lesions.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Prefer irrigation methods that minimize foliage wetness.
- Avoid handling plants while foliage is wet.
- Disinfect tools used for pruning or plant handling where appropriate.
- Remove severely infected plants or plant material according to local recommendations.
- Control volunteer pepper and tomato plants that may harbor the pathogen.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition and overall crop vigor.

### After Harvest
- Remove crop residues where appropriate.
- Control volunteer solanaceous plants.
- Avoid carrying infected plant material into subsequent crops.
- Clean and sanitize tools and equipment where practical.
- Rotate away from susceptible crops when disease pressure is high.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with disease-free seed and transplants.
- Selecting less susceptible cultivars.
- Avoiding overhead irrigation where practical.
- Reducing prolonged leaf wetness.
- Avoiding field operations when plants are wet.
- Maintaining adequate plant spacing.
- Removing infected material.
- Controlling volunteer host plants.
- Cleaning and sanitizing tools.
- Practicing crop rotation.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural and sanitation practices are the main nonchemical components of bacterial spot management.

Useful practices include:
- Disease-free seed and transplants.
- Reduced foliage wetness.
- Removal of infected plants.
- Crop rotation.
- Good field sanitation.
- Avoiding work in wet fields.

Some biological products may be incorporated into regional integrated disease-management programs, but their effectiveness varies with pathogen population, weather, and application timing.

Specific biological-control recommendations are not included in this initial record without current locally applicable evidence.

## Chemical Management

Preventive bactericide programs may be used where bacterial spot is economically important.

Copper-based bactericides are commonly included in bacterial spot management programs, sometimes in combination with other registered products depending on local recommendations.

Chemical management is most effective when:
- Disease risk is identified early.
- Applications are preventive or made at early disease development.
- Coverage of susceptible plant tissues is adequate.
- Cultural practices are used simultaneously.

Reduced sensitivity to copper and other bactericides can occur in some *Xanthomonas* populations. Repeated use of the same chemistry should therefore follow current resistance-management recommendations.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Pepper cultivars vary in their susceptibility to bacterial spot.

Where available, cultivars with improved resistance or reduced susceptibility should be considered, particularly in regions where bacterial spot occurs frequently.

Resistance should be verified for the specific cultivar and local pathogen population.

## Immediate Actions

When pepper bacterial spot is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for small dark water-soaked lesions.
2. Check fruit for raised or corky spots.
3. Review recent rainfall and irrigation conditions.
4. Stop unnecessary overhead irrigation.
5. Avoid handling plants while foliage is wet.
6. Remove severely infected plants where appropriate.
7. Sanitize tools and equipment.
8. Control volunteer pepper and tomato plants.
9. Monitor neighboring plants for new symptoms.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use certified or disease-free seed.
- Start with healthy transplants.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Reduce prolonged leaf wetness.
- Avoid field work while plants are wet.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing.
- Remove infected plant material.
- Control volunteer solanaceous plants.
- Sanitize tools and equipment.
- Rotate away from susceptible crops where practical.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.

## Related Crops

The disease primarily affects:
- Bell pepper
- Hot pepper
- Other *Capsicum* species

Related solanaceous crops such as tomato can also be affected by bacterial spot caused by related *Xanthomonas* populations.
