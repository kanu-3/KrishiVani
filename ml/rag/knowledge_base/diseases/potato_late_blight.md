## Symptoms

Late blight can affect leaves, stems, and tubers.

### Leaves
- Small pale green, water-soaked, or olive-colored spots may initially appear on leaves.
- Lesions enlarge rapidly under cool and wet conditions.
- Lesions become brown to dark brown and irregular in shape.
- Unlike some other leaf-spot diseases, lesions are generally not restricted by leaf veins.
- A pale green or yellowish margin may surround actively developing lesions.
- Under humid or wet conditions, white fungal-like growth may develop around the margins or undersides of lesions.
- Severe infection can cause leaves to rapidly turn brown, collapse, and die.

### Stems and Petioles
- Dark brown to black lesions may develop on petioles and stems.
- Infected stems may become weak and collapse.
- Stem infection can allow the pathogen to spread rapidly through the canopy.

### Tubers
- Tubers may develop irregular, shallow, dull brown, purple-brown, or dark lesions.
- Internal tissue beneath infected areas may develop reddish-brown to brown dry rot.
- Tuber infection can continue after harvest and increase losses during storage.
- Secondary microorganisms may enter infected tubers and accelerate decay.

## Disease Development and Spread

The pathogen is a water mold and not a true fungus.

It can survive and spread through:
- Infected seed tubers
- Infected volunteer potato plants
- Infected tomato plants
- Infected crop debris where conditions permit
- Airborne spores
- Water movement

Spores and other propagules can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Irrigation water
- Movement of infected plant material

During cool, wet weather, the pathogen can produce large numbers of spores on infected tissues. These spores can be carried to nearby healthy plants and initiate new infections.

Disease can progress extremely rapidly when favorable weather persists.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Cool to moderate temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Heavy dew
- Dense foliage
- Cloudy or humid weather
- Presence of infected plants or tubers

The disease can develop particularly quickly when cool temperatures and prolonged wetness occur together. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## Detection

Potato late blight can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop large irregular olive, brown, or dark lesions.
- Lesions are not restricted by leaf veins.
- Wet conditions produce white fungal-like growth around lesions.
- Leaves and stems rapidly turn brown and collapse.
- Tubers develop irregular dull-brown or purple-brown lesions.
- Internal tuber tissue beneath lesions shows reddish-brown to brown discoloration.
- Disease progresses rapidly following cool, wet weather.

Late blight can resemble other potato foliar diseases, so environmental conditions and lesion characteristics should be considered together.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Late blight is one of the most destructive diseases of potato.

It can cause:
- Rapid destruction of foliage
- Stem collapse
- Premature plant death
- Reduced tuber development
- Severe yield loss
- Tuber infection
- Storage rot
- Major economic losses under favorable conditions

Early infection during tuber formation can be particularly damaging. The pathogen can also continue causing losses after harvest when infected tubers enter storage.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use certified disease-free seed potatoes.
- Avoid planting visibly infected tubers.
- Select cultivars with improved late-blight resistance where available.
- Avoid planting potatoes next to known infected potato or tomato crops where practical.
- Use crop rotation where appropriate.
- Control volunteer potatoes that can act as sources of inoculum.
- Obtain planting material from reliable suppliers.

### During Growth
- Scout fields frequently, especially during cool and wet periods.
- Monitor weather and local late-blight forecasting systems where available.
- Remove or manage volunteer potato and infected tomato plants.
- Maintain good field sanitation.
- Avoid unnecessary prolonged foliage wetness.
- Use appropriate fungicide programs when disease risk warrants treatment.
- Monitor susceptible cultivars especially closely.
- Inspect both foliage and stems for rapidly developing lesions.

Late-blight forecasting systems can help identify periods of elevated disease risk and support fungicide decision-making. ([cropprotectionnetwork.org](https://cropprotectionnetwork.org/web-books/fungicide-use-in-field-crops?section=42-decision-making-and-determining-plant-disease-risk&utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### After Harvest
- Avoid leaving cull or infected tubers in the field.
- Remove volunteer potatoes.
- Harvest and handle tubers carefully to minimize wounds.
- Inspect harvested tubers for symptoms.
- Store potatoes under conditions appropriate for reducing disease development and secondary decay.
- Avoid storing visibly infected tubers with healthy marketable tubers.
- Maintain storage sanitation.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Using certified healthy seed tubers.
- Removing volunteer potatoes.
- Removing or managing infected plant material.
- Practicing appropriate crop rotation.
- Monitoring weather and disease forecasts.
- Avoiding prolonged foliage wetness.
- Maintaining good field sanitation.
- Harvesting and storing healthy tubers separately from diseased material.
- Minimizing tuber injury during harvest and handling.

## Biological / Organic Management

There is no established biological treatment that reliably cures a potato plant after severe late-blight infection has become established.

Important nonchemical approaches include:
- Disease-free seed.
- Resistant or less susceptible cultivars.
- Removal of volunteer hosts.
- Field sanitation.
- Disease forecasting and early detection.
- Reducing opportunities for prolonged leaf wetness.

Biological products may be part of some integrated disease-management systems, but their effectiveness varies with product, pathogen pressure, weather, timing, and local regulatory approval.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides are a major component of late-blight management where disease pressure is significant.

Because late blight can progress rapidly, fungicides are generally most effective when applied preventively or very early in disease development, before extensive infection is established. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Late-blight fungicides target water-mold pathogens and should be selected based on:
- Disease risk.
- Crop growth stage.
- Weather conditions.
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Disease forecasting information.
- Local agricultural recommendations.

Fungicide programs should rotate active ingredients or fungicide groups according to current resistance-management recommendations. Resistance has developed in some *P. infestans* populations, making proper product selection and rotation important. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Potato cultivars differ in their susceptibility to late blight.

Cultivars with improved resistance can reduce:
- Disease development
- Epidemic rate
- Fungicide dependence
- Yield losses

Resistance effectiveness can vary depending on the *P. infestans* population present in the region. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Specific cultivar resistance should be verified for the local production environment.

## Immediate Actions

When potato late blight is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for large irregular olive or brown lesions.
2. Check whether lesions are unrestricted by leaf veins.
3. Examine leaves and stems during wet weather for white fungal-like growth.
4. Inspect nearby plants for rapidly developing symptoms.
5. Check for infected volunteer potatoes or tomato plants.
6. Review recent weather conditions and local disease forecasts.
7. Avoid moving infected plant material between fields.
8. Initiate locally recommended disease-management measures promptly.
9. Inspect harvested tubers for internal brown rot.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for confirmation and treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use certified disease-free seed potatoes.
- Select late-blight-resistant or less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Remove volunteer potatoes.
- Avoid planting near known infected host crops where practical.
- Monitor disease forecasts.
- Scout fields frequently during cool, wet weather.
- Minimize prolonged foliage wetness where practical.
- Maintain good field sanitation.
- Begin fungicide protection based on disease risk rather than waiting for severe symptoms.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action.
- Handle harvested tubers carefully.
- Remove visibly infected tubers from storage lots.
