# Apple Cedar Apple Rust

## Crop
Apple

## Crop Aliases
- Apple
- Apple tree
- *Malus domestica*

## Disease
Cedar Apple Rust

## Disease Aliases
- Cedar-apple rust
- Apple rust
- Cedar rust

## Scientific Name
*Gymnosporangium juniperi-virginianae*

## Common Names
- Cedar-apple rust
- Cedar apple rust
- Apple rust

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen requires two different host groups to complete its life cycle:

- Apple
- Crabapple
- Eastern red cedar
- Some susceptible juniper species

Apple and crabapple are the important fruit-tree hosts, while eastern red cedar and certain junipers serve as the alternate hosts.

## Symptoms

Cedar-apple rust can affect apple leaves and fruit. The alternate cedar host develops characteristic woody galls.

### Leaves
- Small pale yellow spots may first appear on the upper surface of leaves.
- Spots enlarge and become yellow to bright orange.
- Lesions may develop a reddish margin.
- Small dark structures may become visible in the center of older leaf lesions.
- Tube-like structures may develop on the underside of infected leaves.
- Severe infection can cause premature leaf drop.

### Fruit
- Young fruit may develop pale green to brown irregular lesions.
- Lesions can become yellow, orange, or brown as they mature.
- Infected areas may become rough or slightly sunken.
- Severe infections can deform developing fruit.
- Fruit lesions generally remain relatively shallow rather than extending deeply into the fruit.

### Cedar / Juniper Hosts
- Round woody galls develop on young branches and twigs.
- Galls are commonly brown to greenish-brown.
- During wet spring weather, orange to yellow gelatinous projections emerge from the galls.
- The gelatinous structures release spores that infect nearby apple and crabapple trees.

## Disease Development and Spread

Cedar-apple rust requires both a susceptible apple-family host and an alternate cedar or juniper host to complete its life cycle.

The fungus overwinters in gall tissue on cedar or juniper hosts.

During warm, wet spring weather, mature galls produce gelatinous orange spore horns. Spores released from these structures are carried by wind to susceptible apple or crabapple foliage and fruit.

After infection of the apple host:
- Leaf and fruit lesions develop.
- Additional spore-producing structures form on infected apple tissue.
- Spores produced on apple leaves are carried back by wind to susceptible cedar or juniper hosts.
- New galls develop on the cedar or juniper host.

The complete disease cycle therefore depends on the presence of both host groups.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Wet spring weather
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Moderate spring temperatures
- Presence of susceptible cedar or juniper hosts near apple trees
- Presence of susceptible apple or crabapple hosts near cedar or juniper

Apple infection is particularly important around the spring bloom period when young leaves and developing fruit are susceptible.

## Detection

Cedar-apple rust can be suspected when:
- Apple leaves develop yellow to bright orange spots.
- Leaf lesions develop reddish margins.
- Dark or tube-like structures appear on mature leaf lesions.
- Young fruit develops irregular green, orange, or brown lesions.
- Nearby eastern red cedar or juniper trees have round woody galls.
- Orange gelatinous projections emerge from cedar or juniper galls after wet spring weather.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Cedar-apple rust is often a minor disease, but susceptible apple cultivars can experience significant infection.

Severe disease can cause:
- Leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic activity
- Reduced fruit size and quality
- Fruit deformation
- Reduced marketability

The presence of susceptible alternate hosts near an apple orchard increases disease risk.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select apple cultivars with resistance or improved tolerance to cedar-apple rust where available.
- Avoid planting susceptible apple cultivars close to heavily infected cedar or juniper hosts.
- Consider the presence of eastern red cedar and susceptible junipers when selecting orchard sites.
- Establish trees with adequate spacing and airflow.

### During Growth
- Inspect apple leaves and fruit regularly during spring.
- Monitor nearby cedar and juniper trees for rust galls.
- Prune and remove cedar or juniper galls before orange spore-producing structures develop where practical.
- Prune infected twigs and branches on apple-family hosts where appropriate.
- Maintain good airflow through proper pruning.
- Avoid prolonged leaf wetness where irrigation is used.

### After Harvest
- Continue monitoring nearby alternate hosts for developing galls.
- Remove severely infected plant material where practical.
- Maintain orchard sanitation.
- Evaluate nearby cedar and juniper hosts before the following spring disease cycle.

Removing or pruning infected cedar galls before they produce spores can reduce the amount of inoculum reaching nearby apple trees.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Selecting resistant or less susceptible apple cultivars.
- Avoiding susceptible apple and cedar/juniper hosts close together where practical.
- Inspecting nearby cedar and juniper trees.
- Removing cedar rust galls before spring spore production.
- Pruning infected twigs where practical.
- Maintaining good airflow around apple trees.
- Monitoring trees during wet spring weather.

## Biological / Organic Management

Physical and cultural management is the main nonchemical approach.

Removing mature rust galls from nearby cedar and juniper hosts before they release spores can reduce disease pressure on apple trees.

Selecting resistant apple cultivars can also reduce the severity of infection.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides can be used preventively where cedar-apple rust is a significant problem and susceptible apple cultivars are being grown.

Protective treatments are most effective when applied before visible rust symptoms develop, particularly during the spring infection period.

Chemical management should be based on:
- Local disease forecasts
- Crop growth stage
- Weather conditions
- Disease history
- Current product labels

Some fungicides used against cedar-apple rust include products based on different fungicide mode-of-action groups. Repeated use of the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance, so products should be rotated according to current resistance-management recommendations.

**Important:** Chemical recommendations, permitted products, formulations, application rates, resistance-management requirements, and regulations vary by country and can change over time. KrishiVani should use current, locally applicable agricultural guidance before providing specific pesticide instructions.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Apple cultivars differ in susceptibility to cedar-apple rust.

Rust-resistant or partially resistant apple and crabapple cultivars are available. Selecting resistant cultivars can substantially reduce disease severity in areas where cedar-apple rust is common.

Resistance should be confirmed for the specific cultivar and local rust population before making a planting decision.

## Immediate Actions

When cedar-apple rust is suspected:

1. Inspect apple leaves for yellow or bright orange spots.
2. Check the underside of leaves for rust structures.
3. Inspect developing fruit for irregular rust lesions.
4. Inspect nearby cedar and juniper trees for woody galls.
5. Remove or prune infected cedar galls before orange gelatinous structures develop where practical.
6. Remove infected apple twigs where appropriate.
7. Improve airflow around susceptible apple trees.
8. Monitor the orchard closely during wet spring weather.
9. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Select cedar-apple-rust-resistant apple cultivars where available.
- Avoid planting susceptible apple and cedar/juniper hosts close together.
- Inspect nearby cedar and juniper trees regularly.
- Remove cedar rust galls before spring spore production where practical.
- Maintain good orchard airflow.
- Avoid unnecessary prolonged leaf wetness.
- Monitor weather and disease-risk periods.
- Use preventive fungicide programs only when justified by local disease risk.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
