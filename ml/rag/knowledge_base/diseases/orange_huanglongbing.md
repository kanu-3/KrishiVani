# Orange Huanglongbing (Citrus Greening)

## Crop
Orange

## Crop Aliases
- Orange
- Sweet orange
- Citrus
- Orange tree
- *Citrus sinensis*

## Disease
Huanglongbing (Citrus Greening)

## Disease Aliases
- Huanglongbing
- HLB
- Citrus greening
- Citrus greening disease
- Yellow shoot disease

## Scientific Name
*Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus* is the principal bacterial pathogen associated with Huanglongbing in many Asian and American citrus-growing regions.

Other *Candidatus Liberibacter* species can also cause HLB in different geographic regions.

## Common Names
- Huanglongbing
- Citrus greening
- Citrus greening disease
- Yellow shoot disease

Huanglongbing affects many citrus species and cultivars, including:
- Sweet orange

- Other susceptible *Citrus* species

## Symptoms

Huanglongbing can affect leaves, shoots, fruit, and the overall tree.

### Leaves
- Leaves may develop irregular yellow and green mottling.
- Mottling is often asymmetrical across the leaf midrib.
- Yellowing may initially occur on individual shoots or sectors of the canopy.
- Leaves may become smaller than normal.
- Leaves may become thicker or leathery.
- Midribs and veins may become enlarged or corky.
- Nutrient-deficiency-like symptoms can occur but typically show an asymmetrical pattern when associated with HLB.

### Shoots and Branches
- Individual shoots may develop yellow leaves.
- New shoots may produce pale green or yellow foliage.
- Twig dieback can occur as the disease progresses.
- The canopy may become sparse.
- Branches may gradually decline and die.

### Fruit
- Fruit may remain partially green even when physiologically mature.
- Fruit may become small and misshapen.
- Fruit may become lopsided or asymmetrical.
- The fruit may show abnormal color development.
- Seeds may be small, poorly developed, or aborted.
- Juice from affected fruit may have a bitter or abnormal taste.
- Premature fruit drop may occur.

### Whole Tree
- Infected trees may show progressive decline.
- Trees may develop sparse foliage.
- Flowering may become irregular or occur outside the normal flowering period.
- Production decreases over time.
- Advanced infection can lead to extensive branch dieback and eventual tree death.

## Disease Development and Spread

Huanglongbing is associated with phloem-inhabiting bacteria and is primarily spread by insect vectors.

The principal vector in many citrus-growing regions is the Asian citrus psyllid (*Diaphorina citri*).

The psyllid acquires the bacterium while feeding on infected citrus and can subsequently transmit it to healthy trees during feeding.

The disease can also be transmitted through infected grafting material.

Infected trees may remain without obvious symptoms for an extended period, allowing the disease to spread before diagnosis.

Once established inside a citrus tree, the pathogen can move through the vascular system and affect multiple parts of the plant.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease spread is favored by:
- Presence of infected citrus trees.
- Presence of Asian citrus psyllids or other competent vectors.
- Abundant young citrus flush.
- Movement of infected plant material.
- Poor detection of infected trees.
- Movement of infected nursery material or grafting material.

The psyllid vector is particularly associated with young citrus shoots and new leaf flush.

## Detection

Huanglongbing can be suspected when:
- Leaves show irregular asymmetrical yellow-green mottling.
- Yellowing begins on individual shoots or sectors of the canopy.
- Leaves become smaller or show corky enlarged veins.
- Young shoots become yellow.
- Fruit becomes small, lopsided, or poorly colored.
- Fruit remains green near the stem while other areas change color.
- Seeds are poorly developed or aborted.
- Premature fruit drop occurs.
- Twig dieback and progressive canopy decline develop.
- Asian citrus psyllids or their nymphs are present on young flush.

HLB symptoms can resemble nutrient deficiencies, especially zinc deficiency. The asymmetrical pattern of blotchy mottling is an important diagnostic clue, but visual symptoms alone are not sufficient for definitive confirmation. ## Disease Severity and Impact

Huanglongbing is one of the most destructive diseases of citrus.

It can cause:
- Progressive tree decline
- Reduced fruit production
- Reduced fruit size
- Poor fruit coloration
- Abnormal fruit shape
- Bitter or poor-quality juice
- Premature fruit drop
- Twig and branch dieback
- Reduced orchard productivity
- Eventual tree death

Infected trees may decline over several years, although the rate of decline varies with cultivar, tree age, pathogen strain, vector pressure, and environmental conditions. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use healthy, certified citrus nursery stock.
- Obtain planting material from reliable and regulated sources.
- Avoid moving citrus plants or propagation material from areas under HLB or vector quarantine without following applicable regulations.
- Use locally recommended rootstocks and cultivars.
- Establish orchards using disease-management practices appropriate to the region.

### During Growth
- Inspect trees regularly for HLB symptoms.
- Monitor young citrus flush for Asian citrus psyllids.
- Remove and manage confirmed infected trees according to current local regulatory and extension recommendations where required.
- Control or suppress vector populations according to locally recommended integrated pest-management programs.
- Maintain tree health through appropriate irrigation and nutrition.
- Avoid moving potentially infected plant material between orchards.
- Monitor neighboring citrus plants because nearby infected trees can act as sources of inoculum.

Maintaining overall tree health is important because HLB-infected trees are under considerable physiological stress, although good nutrition and irrigation do not cure the infection. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

### After Harvest
- Continue monitoring trees for symptoms and vector activity.
- Remove fruit and plant material only according to applicable local regulations and management recommendations.
- Inspect new flush for psyllid activity.
- Maintain sanitation and avoid unauthorized movement of citrus plant material.
- Review orchard disease and vector-management records before the next production cycle.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with certified healthy planting material.
- Monitoring citrus trees regularly.
- Inspecting young flush for psyllids.
- Maintaining good tree vigor.
- Removing confirmed infected trees where recommended.
- Avoiding movement of potentially infected citrus propagation material.
- Maintaining appropriate irrigation.
- Maintaining balanced nutrition.
- Coordinating vector management across neighboring citrus plantings where possible.
- Following applicable quarantine and plant-health regulations.

## Biological / Organic Management

There is currently no established biological treatment that cures an HLB-infected citrus tree.

Nonchemical management focuses primarily on:
- Use of disease-free planting material.
- Early detection.
- Removal or management of infected trees where recommended.
- Reduction of vector populations through integrated pest management.
- Protection of new plantings from infection.
- Maintaining good tree health.

Research continues to investigate biological, genetic, and other approaches to HLB management, but these should not be represented as established cures. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Chemical Management

There is currently no chemical treatment that reliably cures Huanglongbing or eliminates the pathogen from an infected citrus tree.

Chemical control is primarily directed at the insect vector, especially the Asian citrus psyllid.

Where vector control is recommended, insecticide programs should be based on:
- Local pest-management recommendations.
- Psyllid population levels.
- Tree growth stage.
- Presence of young flush.
- Current pesticide labels.
- Resistance-management guidance.

Repeated use of insecticides with the same mode of action can contribute to resistance in psyllid populations. Integrated pest-management programs should therefore rotate modes of action and combine chemical and nonchemical approaches where appropriate.

**Important:** Chemical recommendations, permitted products, formulations, application rates, resistance-management requirements, pre-harvest intervals, and regulations vary by country and can change over time. ## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Complete resistance to Huanglongbing is not generally available in conventional commercial sweet orange cultivars.

Differences in susceptibility and tolerance can occur among citrus species, cultivars, and rootstocks.

Research is ongoing into HLB-tolerant and HLB-resistant citrus varieties and rootstocks. A disease-resistant citrus rootstock developed using genetic engineering was deregulated by USDA APHIS in June 2026, illustrating ongoing progress in this area, but cultivar suitability and regulatory status must be evaluated locally. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Specific resistance or tolerance claims should be verified for the particular cultivar, rootstock, and production region.

## Immediate Actions

When Huanglongbing is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for asymmetrical yellow-green blotchy mottling.
2. Check young shoots for yellowing.
3. Inspect fruit for small size, lopsided shape, abnormal coloration, and premature drop.
4. Examine young flush for Asian citrus psyllids and their nymphs.
5. Photograph and document suspicious symptoms.
6. Do not move potentially infected citrus plants or propagation material.
7. Obtain laboratory or regulatory confirmation where HLB is suspected.
8. Follow applicable quarantine or plant-health reporting requirements.
9. Follow local recommendations for vector management.
10. Consult a qualified citrus pathology or agricultural extension expert before deciding whether infected trees should be removed.

## Prevention

- Use certified disease-free citrus planting material.
- Monitor citrus trees regularly.
- Inspect new flush for Asian citrus psyllids.
- Manage vector populations according to current local recommendations.
- Avoid movement of potentially infected citrus plants or propagation material.
- Follow quarantine and plant-health regulations.
- Remove or manage confirmed infected trees where required or recommended.
- Maintain good tree health through appropriate irrigation and nutrition.
- Coordinate vector management across citrus-growing areas where possible.
- Monitor neighboring trees for symptoms.
