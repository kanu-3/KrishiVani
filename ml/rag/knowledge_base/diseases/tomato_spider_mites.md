## Hosts / Related Crops
The two-spotted spider mite is highly polyphagous and can affect many crops and plants, including:
- Tomato
- Bean
- Cucumber
- Melon
- Strawberry
- Pepper
- Eggplant
- Many vegetable, ornamental, and fruit crops

## Symptoms

Two-spotted spider mites primarily damage leaves by feeding on plant cells.

### Leaves
- Small pale yellow or white speckles may initially appear on leaves.
- Feeding damage produces a stippled appearance across the leaf surface.
- As infestation increases, leaves may become yellow, bronze, gray, or brown.
- Leaves can become dry and brittle.
- Severe feeding can cause premature leaf drop.
- Fine silk webbing may appear on heavily infested leaves.

### Undersides of Leaves
- Mites and eggs are often concentrated on the lower leaf surface.
- Fine webbing may be visible under heavy infestations.
- Small moving mites may be observed with close inspection.

### Whole Plant
- Severe infestations can reduce plant vigor.
- Plants may show stunted growth.
- Fruit production can decline when substantial foliage is damaged.
- Severe infestations can cause extensive defoliation and plant decline.

## Pest Development and Spread

Two-spotted spider mites feed by piercing plant cells and removing their contents.

They can spread through:
- Movement of infested plant material
- Wind
- Contact between plants
- Clothing and equipment
- Workers handling infested plants

Females lay eggs primarily on the undersides of leaves.

Under favorable conditions, spider mites can reproduce very rapidly, allowing populations to increase quickly over a short period.

Hot, dry conditions often favor rapid population growth.

## Favorable Conditions

Infestations are favored by:
- Hot weather
- Dry conditions
- Low relative humidity
- Dusty plant surfaces
- Water-stressed plants
- Reduced natural-enemy activity
- Dense populations of susceptible plants

Spider mite populations can increase rapidly under hot and dry conditions.

Excessive or broad-spectrum insecticide use can also worsen outbreaks by killing predatory mites and other natural enemies.

## Detection

Two-spotted spider mite infestation can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop fine yellow or pale stippling.
- Leaves become bronze or brown as damage increases.
- Fine webbing appears on leaves.
- Small mites are visible on the undersides of leaves.
- Tiny spherical eggs are present among webbing.
- Damage is concentrated during hot, dry weather.
- Plant vigor declines without a clear fungal or bacterial disease.

A hand lens can make mites, eggs, and webbing easier to detect.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Spider mite feeding can cause:
- Leaf stippling
- Chlorosis
- Bronzing
- Leaf drying
- Premature leaf drop
- Reduced photosynthesis
- Reduced plant vigor
- Reduced fruit production
- Severe defoliation under heavy infestation

Early detection is important because populations can increase rapidly under favorable environmental conditions.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Start with healthy tomato transplants.
- Inspect nursery plants for mites and webbing before planting.
- Avoid introducing heavily infested plant material.
- Maintain adequate spacing between plants.
- Establish plants in sites with appropriate irrigation and airflow.

### During Growth
- Inspect the undersides of leaves regularly.
- Use a hand lens to identify mites when symptoms are first noticed.
- Maintain adequate irrigation to avoid plant water stress.
- Use strong but non-damaging water sprays to dislodge mites from foliage where appropriate.
- Remove heavily infested leaves when practical.
- Encourage naturally occurring predatory mites and other beneficial arthropods.
- Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticide applications.
- Monitor nearby plants because infestations can spread.
- Manage dust and plant stress where practical.

### After Harvest
- Remove heavily infested crop residues.
- Clean greenhouse or production areas where appropriate.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants that may support mite populations.
- Inspect nearby host plants for continuing infestations.
- Clean tools and equipment when moving between production areas.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with healthy transplants.
- Regularly inspecting leaf undersides.
- Maintaining adequate irrigation.
- Avoiding plant water stress.
- Removing heavily infested foliage.
- Washing or dislodging mites with suitable water sprays where appropriate.
- Controlling dust around plants where practical.
- Protecting natural enemies.
- Avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticide use.
- Maintaining good crop sanitation.

## Biological / Organic Management

Biological control can be an important component of spider mite management.

Natural enemies that may help suppress two-spotted spider mites include:
- Predatory mites
- Predatory insects
- Other beneficial arthropods

Commercial biological-control programs may use predatory mites such as *Phytoseiulus persimilis* or *Neoseiulus californicus* under suitable production conditions.

The effectiveness of biological control depends on:
- Mite population density.
- Temperature.
- Humidity.
- Availability of prey.
- Timing of predator release.
- Compatibility with other pest-control measures.

Avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticides is important because these products can reduce beneficial predator populations.

## Chemical Management

Miticides or insecticides may be used when spider mite populations reach economically significant levels.

Chemical treatment should not be based solely on leaf discoloration because several plant stresses can produce similar symptoms.

Management decisions should consider:
- Confirmed mite presence.
- Population density.
- Plant growth stage.
- Severity of damage.
- Presence of natural enemies.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

Spider mites can develop resistance rapidly to repeated use of the same active ingredient or mode of action. Different miticide modes of action should therefore be rotated according to current resistance-management guidance.

Broad-spectrum insecticides can sometimes worsen spider mite outbreaks by destroying natural enemies.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato varieties are not generally selected for complete resistance to two-spotted spider mites.

Differences in plant vigor, leaf characteristics, and environmental adaptation may influence susceptibility, but these should not be interpreted as reliable genetic resistance.

Effective management therefore depends primarily on:
- Monitoring.
- Maintaining plant health.
- Biological control.
- Cultural practices.
- Appropriate chemical intervention when necessary.

## Immediate Actions

When two-spotted spider mites are suspected:

1. Inspect the undersides of affected leaves.
2. Look for tiny moving mites and eggs.
3. Check for fine webbing.
4. Inspect neighboring tomato plants.
5. Assess recent hot and dry weather conditions.
6. Maintain adequate irrigation to reduce plant water stress.
7. Remove heavily infested leaves where practical.
8. Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticide use.
9. Encourage or conserve predatory mites and other natural enemies.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert if the infestation is increasing rapidly.

## Prevention

- Use healthy tomato transplants.
- Inspect plants before planting.
- Monitor leaf undersides regularly.
- Maintain adequate irrigation.
- Avoid water-stressed plants.
- Reduce dusty conditions where practical.
- Encourage natural enemies.
- Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticides.
- Remove heavily infested plant material.
- Monitor surrounding host plants.
- Detect infestations early before populations increase rapidly.
- Rotate miticide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
