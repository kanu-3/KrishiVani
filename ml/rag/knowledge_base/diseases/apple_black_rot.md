# Apple Black Rot

## Crop
Apple

## Crop Aliases
- Apple
- Apple tree
- *Malus domestica*

## Disease
Black Rot

## Disease Aliases
- Apple black rot
- Black rot of apple
- Frogeye leaf spot
- Black rot canker

## Scientific Name
*Diplodia seriata*

## Common Names
- Apple black rot
- Black rot
- Frogeye leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen primarily affects apple and crabapple species (*Malus*).

## Symptoms

Black rot can affect leaves, branches, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Leaves may develop circular spots with purple or reddish margins and tan to brown centers.
- These lesions are commonly known as frogeye leaf spots.
- Lesions may enlarge and become more noticeable as the season progresses.
- Severe infection can cause affected leaves to yellow and fall prematurely.

### Branches
- Branch cankers may appear as sunken, reddish-brown areas.
- Older cankers may become rough, cracked, or enlarged.
- Cankers can occur on damaged or weakened branches.
- Branches beyond a canker may wilt, die back, and lose their leaves.

### Fruit
- Brown rotting areas may develop on fruit, often near the blossom end.
- Larger lesions may develop concentric rings that become brown to black.
- The fruit flesh may remain relatively firm while the rot develops.
- Small black fungal structures may develop on older fruit lesions.
- Infected fruit may shrivel, dry, and remain attached to the tree as mummified fruit.
- Severe infections can cause premature fruit rot and reduce marketable yield.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus can survive in:
- Branch cankers
- Dead or damaged wood
- Mummified fruit
- Other infected plant material

During wet weather, fungal spores are released from infected tissues.

Spores can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Splashing irrigation water

The pathogen can enter leaves and fruit through natural openings or minor wounds. Branches and trunks are particularly susceptible where tissue has been damaged by winter injury, pruning wounds, fire blight, drought stress, or other environmental injuries.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Wet weather
- High humidity
- Prolonged moisture on plant surfaces
- Dead or damaged wood
- Winter injury
- Drought or other tree stress
- Waterlogged or poorly drained conditions
- Existing fire blight damage

Stressed or damaged trees are generally more susceptible to black rot infection.

## Detection

Apple black rot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop circular spots with purple or reddish margins and tan centers.
- Branches develop sunken reddish-brown cankers.
- Fruit develops brown to black rot with concentric rings.
- Mummified fruit remains attached to the tree.
- Branches beyond a canker wilt or die back.
- Symptoms occur in association with dead or damaged wood.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Black rot can cause:
- Leaf spotting and premature leaf loss
- Branch cankers
- Branch dieback
- Fruit rot
- Mummified fruit
- Reduced fruit quality
- Reduced marketable yield

Severe disease can become more damaging when trees are already weakened by environmental stress or other diseases.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select healthy planting material from reliable sources.
- Choose cultivars adapted to local growing conditions.
- Establish trees in sites with good drainage.
- Avoid planting trees where environmental stress is likely to be severe.
- Maintain adequate spacing for airflow.

### During Growth
- Inspect trees regularly for leaf spots, cankers, and fruit rot.
- Prune out dead and diseased branches.
- Remove branches killed by fire blight where appropriate.
- Avoid unnecessary injury to trunks and branches.
- Maintain adequate irrigation during drought conditions.
- Improve drainage where excessive soil moisture stresses trees.
- Maintain good airflow through appropriate pruning.
- Remove mummified fruit from trees.

### After Harvest
- Remove remaining mummified fruit.
- Remove dead or diseased branches.
- Remove infected plant material from the orchard.
- Remove dead stumps where they can serve as sources of fungal inoculum.
- Maintain orchard sanitation.

Removing dead wood and mummified fruit reduces potential sources of spores for future infections.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Pruning dead and diseased wood.
- Removing mummified fruit.
- Removing infected plant material.
- Maintaining good tree vigor.
- Preventing unnecessary mechanical injury.
- Managing drought stress.
- Improving drainage in poorly drained sites.
- Controlling other diseases that produce dead wood, such as fire blight.
- Maintaining good orchard sanitation.

## Biological / Organic Management

Sanitation is the primary nonchemical management approach.

Removing:
- Dead branches
- Diseased wood
- Mummified fruit
- Other infected plant material

can reduce the amount of fungal inoculum available for future infection.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides are generally not the first management option for black rot when effective sanitation and tree-care practices can be maintained.

Where chemical control is justified, preventive fungicide programs may help reduce leaf and fruit infections. Some fungicides used for apple disease management may provide activity against black rot.

Chemical treatment should be integrated with sanitation because fungicides do not remove existing branch cankers or infected wood.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Products should therefore be selected and rotated according to current local recommendations and resistance-management guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Apple cultivars can differ in their susceptibility to black rot.

Where information is available, selecting cultivars adapted to local environmental conditions and maintaining good tree health can reduce disease risk.

Complete resistance should not be assumed for any cultivar unless supported by current varietal or extension information.

## Immediate Actions

When black rot is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for characteristic frogeye leaf spots.
2. Check branches for sunken reddish-brown cankers.
3. Inspect fruit for brown or black rotting lesions.
4. Remove mummified fruit from the tree.
5. Prune out dead or diseased branches where practical.
6. Remove infected plant material from the orchard.
7. Reduce tree stress caused by drought or poor drainage.
8. Monitor nearby trees for similar symptoms.
9. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Start with healthy planting material.
- Maintain good tree vigor.
- Avoid unnecessary injury to trunks and branches.
- Prune dead and diseased wood.
- Remove mummified fruit.
- Remove infected plant material.
- Manage drought stress.
- Maintain adequate drainage.
- Improve airflow through proper pruning.
- Monitor for fire blight and remove associated dead wood where appropriate.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations when chemical control is necessary.

The pathogen primarily affects:
