# Squash Powdery Mildew

## Crop
Squash

## Crop Aliases
- Squash
- Summer squash
- Winter squash
- Pumpkin
- *Cucurbita* species

## Disease
Powdery Mildew

## Disease Aliases
- Squash powdery mildew
- Powdery mildew of squash
- Cucurbit powdery mildew
- Powdery mildew of cucurbits

## Scientific Name
*Podosphaera xanthii* and other powdery mildew species can cause powdery mildew of cucurbits.

## Common Names
- Powdery mildew
- Cucurbit powdery mildew
- Squash powdery mildew

## Hosts / Related Crops
The disease can affect many cucurbit crops, including:
- Squash
- Pumpkin
- Cucumber
- Melon
- Watermelon
- Other susceptible *Cucurbita* species

## Symptoms

Powdery mildew primarily affects leaves and other green plant tissues.

### Leaves
- Small white powdery patches may initially appear on leaf surfaces.
- White fungal growth can enlarge and cover substantial portions of the leaf.
- Infected leaves may become yellow or pale beneath the fungal growth.
- Severely infected leaves may become brown and necrotic.
- Leaves can dry and die prematurely under heavy disease pressure.
- Infection can reduce the functional green leaf area.

### Petioles and Stems
- White powdery growth may develop on petioles and young stems.
- Severe infection can weaken green tissues and reduce overall plant vigor.

### Fruit
- Fruit is generally less directly affected than leaves.
- Severe foliar infection can indirectly reduce fruit size, quality, or development by reducing photosynthetic activity.

## Disease Development and Spread

Powdery mildew fungi produce spores on infected plant surfaces.

Spores spread primarily through:
- Wind
- Air movement
- Contact with infected plant material

Unlike many foliar fungal diseases, powdery mildew does not require free water on the leaf surface for infection.

The disease can spread rapidly once colonies become established, especially when susceptible young foliage is continuously produced.

The pathogen can survive between cropping cycles on infected plant material or suitable host plants, depending on the species and local environment.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High relative humidity
- Dense foliage
- Poor air circulation
- Abundant succulent growth
- High nitrogen availability
- Presence of susceptible host plants

Powdery mildew can develop even when foliage is not continuously wet because free water is generally not required for infection.

## Detection

Squash powdery mildew can be suspected when:
- White powdery growth appears on leaves.
- Powdery patches expand across leaf surfaces.
- Infected leaves become yellow beneath colonies.
- Severe leaves become brown and necrotic.
- New growth repeatedly develops similar white fungal patches.
- Disease increases under warm, humid conditions.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Powdery mildew can cause:
- Reduced photosynthetic leaf area
- Premature leaf senescence
- Reduced plant vigor
- Reduced fruit size
- Reduced fruit quality
- Reduced yield under severe or early-season infection

Severe disease is more damaging when substantial foliage is infected before or during fruit development.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select squash varieties with resistance or improved tolerance to powdery mildew where available.
- Start with healthy seed and planting material.
- Avoid selecting highly susceptible cultivars in areas with a history of severe disease.
- Provide adequate spacing between plants.
- Choose sites with good sunlight and airflow.

### During Growth
- Inspect leaves regularly for early powdery mildew colonies.
- Maintain adequate spacing and airflow.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen fertilization that produces highly succulent growth.
- Remove severely infected leaves where practical.
- Manage weeds that create dense humid conditions.
- Avoid unnecessary overcrowding.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition.
- Monitor disease development during warm, humid weather.

### After Harvest
- Remove crop residues according to local production practices.
- Remove heavily infected plant material.
- Control volunteer cucurbit plants where practical.
- Review cultivar performance before the following season.
- Maintain field sanitation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be used when powdery mildew becomes economically important.

Management is generally more effective when treatment begins early, before severe colonization has developed.

Fungicide choices should consider:
- Crop growth stage.
- Disease severity.
- Weather conditions.
- Variety susceptibility.
- Local disease-management recommendations.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Powdery mildew populations can develop resistance to several fungicide groups, so rotation and integrated management are important.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Squash and other cucurbit cultivars differ significantly in susceptibility to powdery mildew.

Disease-resistant or tolerant cultivars can substantially reduce disease severity and the need for chemical intervention.

Resistance should be verified for the specific cultivar and local powdery mildew population because resistance performance can vary between regions.

## Immediate Actions

When squash powdery mildew is suspected:

1. Inspect upper and lower leaf surfaces for white powdery colonies.
2. Check young leaves and shoots for newly developing symptoms.
3. Remove severely infected foliage where practical.
4. Improve airflow around plants.
5. Avoid excessive nitrogen fertilization.
6. Reduce unnecessary canopy density.
7. Monitor nearby cucurbit plants for similar symptoms.
8. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Select powdery-mildew-resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Use healthy seed and planting material.
- Maintain adequate spacing between plants.
- Improve airflow through appropriate plant management.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen.
- Control weeds.
- Monitor plants regularly.
- Remove severely infected foliage where practical.
- Maintain balanced nutrition.
- Start chemical management early when disease risk justifies treatment.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
