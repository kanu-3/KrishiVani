# Tomato Septoria Leaf Spot

## Crop
Tomato

## Crop Aliases
- Tomato
- Tomato plant
- *Solanum lycopersicum*

## Disease
Septoria Leaf Spot

## Disease Aliases
- Tomato Septoria leaf spot
- Septoria spot
- Septoria leaf spot of tomato
- Septoria blight

## Scientific Name
*Septoria lycopersici*

## Common Names
- Septoria leaf spot
- Tomato Septoria leaf spot
- Septoria blight

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen primarily affects:
- Tomato
- *Solanum lycopersicum*

Other related solanaceous plants can potentially harbor the pathogen and contribute to disease pressure.

## Symptoms

Septoria leaf spot primarily affects leaves but can also occur on stems, petioles, and sepals.

### Leaves
- Small circular spots initially appear on older lower leaves.
- Lesions commonly have dark brown to black margins.
- Centers are usually tan, gray, or pale brown.
- Small black dots may develop in the center of older lesions.
- The black dots are fungal fruiting structures called pycnidia.
- Lesions can increase in number and merge when disease pressure is high.
- Severely affected leaves become yellow, brown, and necrotic.
- Infected leaves may eventually fall from the plant.
- Disease generally progresses upward from lower foliage as the season continues.

### Stems and Petioles
- Small dark lesions may occasionally develop on stems and petioles.
- Severe infection can weaken affected tissues.

### Sepals
- Small lesions may develop on sepals and surrounding flower structures.

### Fruit
- Fruit is generally not a major site of infection.
- Unlike bacterial spot or early blight, Septoria leaf spot usually does not produce characteristic fruit lesions.
- Severe defoliation can indirectly reduce fruit development and quality by reducing functional leaf area.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus survives primarily in:
- Infected tomato debris
- Infected plant material
- Infected weeds belonging to susceptible host groups
- Potentially contaminated seed

Spores are produced inside pycnidia in infected leaf tissue.

Spores spread primarily through:
- Rain splash
- Irrigation water
- Wind-driven water
- Contact with wet infected plants
- Contaminated hands, clothing, tools, and equipment

The pathogen commonly begins infecting lower leaves after spores splash upward from contaminated plant debris or soil.

Repeated wetting can produce multiple infection cycles during the growing season.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Moderate temperatures
- Dense foliage
- Poor air circulation
- Presence of infected plant debris

The disease is particularly common when foliage remains wet for extended periods. University of Minnesota Extension reports that Septoria leaf spot is favored by high humidity and temperatures around 68–77°F (20–25°C). :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Detection

Tomato Septoria leaf spot can be suspected when:
- Small circular spots appear first on lower older leaves.
- Spots have dark margins and tan or gray centers.
- Tiny black dots appear in the center of mature lesions.
- Disease progresses upward through the plant.
- Severe infection causes yellowing and premature leaf drop.
- Symptoms become more severe following wet weather.

The presence of small black pycnidia in the center of tan or gray lesions is particularly useful for distinguishing Septoria leaf spot from some other tomato leaf-spot diseases. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Disease Severity and Impact

Septoria leaf spot can cause:
- Progressive leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Reduced plant vigor
- Reduced fruit size or ripening performance when severe
- Reduced yield under heavy or early-season infection

Although fruit is usually not directly infected, extensive foliage loss can reduce the plant's ability to support fruit development.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use healthy tomato seed and transplants.
- Avoid saving seed from heavily infected plants.
- Select cultivars with improved disease tolerance where available.
- Choose planting sites with good airflow and drainage.
- Avoid planting tomatoes immediately after severely diseased tomato crops where practical.
- Remove susceptible weeds that can harbor the pathogen.

### During Growth
- Scout lower leaves regularly for early symptoms.
- Remove infected leaves where practical.
- Avoid working with tomato plants while foliage is wet.
- Use drip irrigation or direct water to the soil surface.
- Stake or trellis plants to improve airflow.
- Remove lower leaves where appropriate to reduce soil-to-foliage contact.
- Use mulch to reduce soil and debris splash onto lower foliage.
- Remove weeds around tomato plants.
- Sanitize pruning and cutting tools when working with infected plants.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition.

University of Minnesota Extension recommends staking or trellising, removing infected leaves, mulching, keeping foliage dry, and rotating tomatoes and peppers away from the same site for disease management. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

### After Harvest
- Remove tomato plants and infected debris from the field.
- Do not leave heavily infected plant material near the next tomato crop.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants.
- Control susceptible weeds.
- Rotate tomatoes and peppers away from the same location where practical.
- Sanitize reusable tools and equipment.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with healthy seed and transplants.
- Avoiding seed from infected plants.
- Maintaining good airflow.
- Staking or trellising plants.
- Removing infected and lower leaves where appropriate.
- Applying mulch to reduce soil splash.
- Using drip or base irrigation.
- Avoiding work in wet foliage.
- Controlling susceptible weeds.
- Removing infected crop debris.
- Practicing crop rotation.

Keeping foliage dry and reducing splash from contaminated soil or plant debris are especially important because the pathogen spreads readily through water movement. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural and sanitation practices are the main nonchemical management tools.

Useful approaches include:
- Removing infected leaves.
- Removing crop debris.
- Improving airflow.
- Using mulch.
- Reducing foliage wetness.
- Using healthy planting material.
- Practicing crop rotation.
- Controlling susceptible weeds.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be considered when Septoria leaf spot is severe, recurrent, or threatens significant foliage loss.

Chemical management is generally more useful when applied preventively or early in disease development rather than after extensive defoliation has occurred.

Treatment decisions should consider:
- Disease severity.
- Weather conditions.
- Crop growth stage.
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Local agricultural recommendations.

In many home-garden situations, cultural practices are sufficient to manage tomato leaf-spot diseases, and fungicides may not be necessary. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Where fungicides are used, products should be rotated according to current resistance-management recommendations. Repeated use of the same fungicide mode of action can increase the risk of resistance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato cultivars can differ in their susceptibility to Septoria leaf spot, although complete resistance is not generally available across commercial tomato varieties.

Where locally available, cultivars with improved tolerance may reduce disease severity.

Cultivar performance should be verified for the local growing environment and disease pressure.

## Immediate Actions

When Septoria leaf spot is suspected:

1. Inspect the oldest lower leaves first.
2. Look for small circular spots with dark margins and tan or gray centers.
3. Check lesion centers for tiny black pycnidia.
4. Remove infected leaves where practical.
5. Improve airflow around the plant.
6. Avoid overhead irrigation.
7. Apply mulch to reduce soil and plant-debris splash.
8. Avoid working with wet tomato foliage.
9. Remove infected plant debris after harvest.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert when diagnosis is uncertain.

## Prevention

- Use healthy seed and transplants.
- Do not save seed from infected plants.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Maintain good airflow.
- Stake or trellis tomato plants.
- Remove lower leaves where appropriate.
- Keep foliage as dry as practical.
- Use drip or base irrigation.
- Apply mulch to reduce splash from soil and crop debris.
- Control susceptible weeds.
- Remove infected crop debris after harvest.
- Rotate tomatoes and peppers away from the same planting location where practical.
- Sanitize tools and equipment.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
