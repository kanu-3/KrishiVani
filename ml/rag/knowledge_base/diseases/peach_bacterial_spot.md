# Peach Bacterial Spot

## Crop
Peach

## Crop Aliases
- Peach
- Peach tree
- *Prunus persica*

## Disease
Bacterial Spot

## Disease Aliases
- Peach bacterial spot
- Bacterial spot of peach
- Bacterial spot of stone fruit
- Bacterial spot of peach and nectarine

## Scientific Name
*Xanthomonas arboricola* pv. *pruni*

## Common Names
- Peach bacterial spot
- Bacterial spot
- Bacterial spot of peach

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen can affect several stone-fruit hosts, including:
- Peach
- Nectarine
- Plum
- Apricot
- Some other susceptible *Prunus* species

Peach and nectarine can be particularly susceptible to economically important disease.

## Symptoms

Bacterial spot can affect leaves, fruit, and young twigs.

### Leaves
- Small water-soaked spots may initially appear on leaves.
- Lesions become dark brown to black as infected tissue dies.
- Spots may develop primarily near the leaf margins, tips, or midrib.
- Individual lesions may enlarge and merge.
- Dead tissue may eventually fall out, producing small holes resembling shot-hole injury.
- Severe infection can cause yellowing and premature leaf drop.

### Fruit
- Small water-soaked spots may first develop on immature fruit.
- Lesions become dark brown to black and may enlarge slightly.
- Fruit lesions can become sunken.
- Multiple lesions may occur on a single fruit.
- Infected areas may exude amber-colored gum.
- Severe fruit infection can cause fruit cracking, distortion, reduced marketability, and premature fruit drop.

### Shoots and Twigs
- Small elongated lesions or cankers may develop on young twigs.
- Twig infections can provide sites where the bacterium survives between growing seasons.
- Severe infection can weaken affected shoots.

## Disease Development and Spread

The bacterium can survive on infected plant tissues and overwintering sites such as infected fruit, twigs, and other plant material.

During favorable weather, bacteria spread from infected tissues to susceptible leaves and fruit.

Spread occurs primarily through:
- Rain splash
- Wind-driven rain
- Water movement
- Irrigation water
- Contact with infected plant material

The pathogen enters plant tissues through natural openings or wounds.

Warm, wet conditions favor bacterial multiplication, movement, and infection. Disease pressure can increase rapidly when susceptible foliage and fruit are repeatedly exposed to wet weather. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Wind-driven rain
- Overhead irrigation
- Wounds or natural openings on susceptible tissues
- Presence of infected plant material

Young developing tissues can be particularly susceptible during periods of active growth.

## Detection

Peach bacterial spot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop small brown to black spots.
- Some leaf lesions fall out and produce shot-hole-like symptoms.
- Fruit develops small dark, sunken lesions.
- Amber-colored gum appears around fruit lesions.
- Multiple fruit lesions occur during warm, wet weather.
- Young twigs show small dark lesions.
- Symptoms increase following repeated rainfall or prolonged foliage wetness.

Because other bacterial, fungal, and insect-related injuries can produce similar symptoms, diagnosis should consider symptom distribution and recent weather conditions.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Peach bacterial spot can cause:
- Leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Fruit spotting and scarring
- Fruit cracking
- Premature fruit drop
- Reduced marketable yield
- Reduced fruit quality

Severe disease can cause significant economic losses, particularly in susceptible cultivars and orchards experiencing prolonged favorable weather. Penn State's 2026 disease update notes that significant yield loss can occur depending on disease pressure and cultivar susceptibility. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select healthy planting material from reliable nurseries.
- Choose cultivars with lower susceptibility to bacterial spot where suitable options are available.
- Select planting sites with good air circulation and drainage.
- Consider historical bacterial spot pressure when selecting cultivars and orchard locations.
- Avoid unnecessary movement of infected plant material.

### During Growth
- Scout trees regularly for leaf and fruit lesions.
- Monitor weather conditions that favor bacterial infection.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Use irrigation methods that minimize prolonged foliage wetness where practical.
- Maintain good canopy airflow.
- Remove severely diseased plant material where appropriate.
- Maintain balanced tree nutrition and adequate tree vigor.
- Monitor susceptible cultivars closely during periods of frequent rainfall.

### After Harvest
- Remove heavily infected or mummified fruit where practical.
- Maintain orchard sanitation.
- Remove severely diseased plant material where appropriate.
- Review cultivar susceptibility and orchard disease history before the next growing season.
- Plan preventive management before periods of expected spring disease activity.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural management is an important component of bacterial spot control.

Useful practices include:
- Selecting less susceptible cultivars.
- Maintaining good tree vigor.
- Improving air circulation.
- Reducing prolonged foliage wetness.
- Using healthy planting material.
- Removing infected material where practical.

Some biological products may be included in regional disease-management programs, but their effectiveness depends on product, timing, pathogen population, and local conditions.

## Chemical Management

Bactericides may be used preventively where bacterial spot is an economically important disease.

Current regional recommendations may include copper-based products and selected biological or bactericidal products, with timing based on:
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Disease history.
- Weather conditions.
- Crop developmental stage.
- Local infection risk.

Penn State's 2026 guidance specifically identifies bacterial spot as an important disease of susceptible peach and other stone fruits and recommends management programs during the susceptible period. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Repeated use of copper or other bactericidal products should follow local resistance-management guidance because bacterial populations can develop reduced sensitivity to copper.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Peach cultivars differ considerably in susceptibility to bacterial spot.

Selecting cultivars with improved resistance or lower susceptibility can reduce disease severity and the need for chemical intervention.

Cultivar resistance should be verified for the specific variety and local production environment because disease pressure and pathogen populations can differ between regions.

## Immediate Actions

When peach bacterial spot is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for small brown or black lesions.
2. Check whether leaf lesions have produced shot-hole-like openings.
3. Inspect developing fruit for dark, sunken spots.
4. Check fruit lesions for amber-colored gum.
5. Inspect young twigs for dark lesions.
6. Review recent rainfall and humidity conditions.
7. Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
8. Improve canopy airflow where practical.
9. Monitor disease progression on susceptible trees.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for confirmation and treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use healthy nursery material.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Maintain good canopy airflow.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Reduce prolonged foliage wetness.
- Monitor trees during warm, wet weather.
- Maintain good tree vigor.
- Remove infected plant material where appropriate.
- Maintain orchard sanitation.
- Begin preventive disease-management programs according to current local recommendations.
- Rotate or integrate bactericidal modes of action where appropriate.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide regulations.
