## Symptoms

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus can affect leaves, shoots, flowers, fruit development, and overall plant growth.

### Leaves
- Young leaves become smaller than normal.
- Leaves curl upward along the margins.
- Leaves may become strongly crumpled or distorted.
- Interveinal and marginal yellowing can develop.
- Leaf surfaces may become thickened or abnormal.
- Severe infection can result in extensive yellowing and curling.
- Symptoms are usually more pronounced on newly developing foliage.

### Shoots and Plant Growth
- Plants may become severely stunted, particularly when infection occurs at an early growth stage.
- Internodes become shortened.
- Plants can develop a compact, upright, bushy appearance.
- New growth may remain small and distorted.
- Severe plants may show very limited further growth.

### Flowers and Fruit
- Flowers formed on infected plants may fail to develop normally.
- Flowers may drop before successful fruit set.
- Fruit production can be greatly reduced.
- Early infection can result in severe loss of yield because plants fail to produce normal vegetative growth and reproductive structures.
- Plants infected later in development may continue producing some fruit, but yield and quality can still be reduced.

## Disease Development and Spread

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus is primarily transmitted by whiteflies.

The main vector is:
- *Bemisia tabaci* species complex

Whiteflies acquire the virus while feeding on infected plants and can transmit it to healthy tomato plants during subsequent feeding.

The virus can spread through:
- Viruliferous whiteflies
- Infected tomato transplants
- Movement of infected plant material
- Infected weed hosts
- Whiteflies carried on transported plants or plant material

Short-distance spread within fields is primarily associated with whiteflies. Long-distance spread can occur through movement of infected transplants and, under some circumstances, movement of viruliferous whiteflies.

Plants can remain apparently symptomless for a period after infection, allowing infected transplants to move the virus unnoticed. UC IPM notes that symptom development can take up to several weeks after infection. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Favorable Conditions

Disease spread is favored by:
- High populations of whitefly vectors.
- Warm growing conditions that support whitefly development.
- Abundant young tomato growth.
- Presence of infected tomato plants.
- Presence of infected weed or alternative host plants.
- Movement of infected transplants.
- Overlapping tomato crops that maintain virus and vector populations.

The disease risk can increase when susceptible tomato plants are grown continuously in areas where the whitefly vector and virus are established.

## Detection

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus can be suspected when:
- Young leaves become small and curl upward.
- Leaves show strong yellowing between or along the veins.
- New foliage becomes crumpled and distorted.
- Internodes become unusually short.
- Plants develop a compact, bushy or upright appearance.
- Flowers fail to develop normally and fall.
- Plants become severely stunted.
- Symptoms occur together with whitefly activity.

The characteristic combination of upward leaf curling, yellowing, shortened internodes, and severe stunting is highly suggestive of TYLCV, but visual symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish all begomoviruses. Laboratory testing may be required for confirmation. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Disease Severity and Impact

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus can cause:
- Severe plant stunting
- Leaf curling and yellowing
- Reduced leaf area
- Flower abortion
- Poor fruit set
- Major yield reduction
- Reduced fruit production
- Severe crop loss when infection occurs early

Plants infected at an early stage generally suffer much greater damage than plants infected later in the growing season. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use virus-free and whitefly-free tomato transplants.
- Purchase planting material from reliable and regulated nurseries.
- Select TYLCV-resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Do not introduce tomato transplants from areas known to have significant TYLCV pressure without following applicable plant-health regulations.
- Avoid planting next to known infected tomato fields where practical.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing.

### During Growth
- Monitor plants regularly for early TYLCV symptoms.
- Monitor young tomato flush for whiteflies.
- Manage whitefly populations using an integrated pest-management program.
- Remove and destroy clearly infected plants where recommended, particularly when disease incidence is low and removal can reduce inoculum.
- Control weeds that may harbor the virus or vector.
- Avoid overlapping old and new tomato crops where practical.
- Use fine-mesh floating row covers for young plants where locally appropriate.
- Maintain good crop vigor through appropriate irrigation and nutrition.

UC IPM specifically recommends resistant varieties, virus- and whitefly-free transplants, whitefly management, removal of infected plants at low incidence, weed management, and avoiding overlap with older tomato fields. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

### After Harvest
- Remove and destroy old tomato crop residue promptly.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants.
- Manage weeds that can support whiteflies or virus.
- Avoid leaving infected tomato plants in production areas.
- Coordinate host-free periods where locally recommended.
- Inspect nearby crops and plantings before establishing the next tomato crop.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with virus-free and vector-free planting material.
- Selecting resistant or tolerant cultivars.
- Monitoring whiteflies.
- Removing infected plants where appropriate.
- Controlling weeds.
- Avoiding overlapping tomato crops.
- Using physical barriers such as fine-mesh covers for young plants where appropriate.
- Maintaining good crop sanitation.
- Removing crop residues promptly after harvest.
- Preventing movement of infected planting material.

## Biological / Organic Management

There is no biological treatment that cures a tomato plant after TYLCV infection has become established.

Important nonchemical approaches include:
- Virus-free planting material.
- Resistant or tolerant cultivars.
- Whitefly exclusion using suitable mesh covers.
- Removal of infected plants where appropriate.
- Weed management.
- Crop sanitation.
- Host-free periods where locally appropriate.
- Conservation of beneficial organisms that naturally suppress whiteflies.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because their effectiveness depends on vector density, environmental conditions, production system, and locally applicable integrated pest-management recommendations.

## Chemical Management

There is no pesticide treatment that cures TYLCV-infected tomato plants.

Chemical management is directed primarily at the whitefly vector.

Whitefly-management programs should consider:
- Vector population density.
- Crop growth stage.
- Presence of virus-infected plants.
- Current local integrated pest-management recommendations.
- Insecticide resistance-management requirements.

Repeated use of the same insecticide mode of action can contribute to resistance in whitefly populations. Insecticides should therefore be rotated according to current resistance-management guidance and integrated with nonchemical measures.

Chemical control of the vector should not be represented as a cure for infected plants.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

TYLCV-resistant and tolerant tomato cultivars are important tools for managing the disease.

Resistance varies among cultivars and may depend on the virus strain or begomovirus population present in a region.

Where available:
- Prefer cultivars with documented resistance or tolerance to locally important TYLCV populations.
- Verify resistance claims for the specific cultivar and production region.
- Combine genetic resistance with vector management because resistance does not necessarily eliminate infection under high disease pressure.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato yellow leaf curl virus is suspected:

1. Inspect young leaves for upward curling and yellowing.
2. Check whether new leaves are small, crumpled, or distorted.
3. Examine plants for shortened internodes and severe stunting.
4. Inspect flowers for abnormal development or premature drop.
5. Check the underside of young leaves for whiteflies.
6. Isolate or mark suspicious plants where practical.
7. Avoid moving potentially infected plants between production areas.
8. Remove clearly infected plants according to local recommendations when disease incidence is low and removal is appropriate.
9. Control whiteflies using current integrated pest-management guidance.
10. Seek laboratory confirmation where TYLCV or another begomovirus is suspected.

## Prevention

- Use virus-free, whitefly-free transplants.
- Select TYLCV-resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Monitor whitefly populations regularly.
- Manage whiteflies with integrated pest-management practices.
- Use fine-mesh exclusion covers for young plants where appropriate.
- Remove infected plants where recommended.
- Control weeds and volunteer tomato plants.
- Avoid overlapping tomato crops.
- Remove old crop residue promptly after harvest.
- Avoid moving potentially infected planting material.
- Maintain good crop sanitation.
- Follow current local plant-health and agricultural recommendations.
