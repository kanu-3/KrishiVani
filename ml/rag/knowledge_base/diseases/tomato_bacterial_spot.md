# Tomato Bacterial Spot

Tomato

Bacterial Spot

## Scientific Name
*Xanthomonas* species complex

## Common Names
- Bacterial spot

- Bacterial leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The disease can affect:
- Tomato
- Bell pepper
- Hot pepper
- Other susceptible *Solanum* and *Capsicum* hosts

## Symptoms

Bacterial spot can affect leaves, stems, petioles, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small water-soaked spots may initially appear on young or mature leaves.
- Lesions become dark brown to black as infected tissue dies.
- Spots may remain relatively small but can enlarge and merge under severe disease pressure.
- Lesions may have irregular or angular margins.
- Severely infected leaves may yellow and drop prematurely.
- Heavy infection can produce extensive necrotic areas across the foliage.

### Stems and Petioles
- Small dark lesions may develop on stems and petioles.
- Lesions can become elongated or irregular.
- Severe infection can weaken affected tissues.
- Heavy stem and petiole infection can contribute to premature leaf loss.

### Fruit
- Small water-soaked spots may first appear on immature green fruit.
- Lesions become dark, raised, rough, and scabby as they develop.
- Fruit lesions may become surrounded by a slightly lighter green halo.
- Multiple lesions can occur on individual fruit.
- Severe infection can reduce fruit quality and marketability.

## Disease Development and Spread

The pathogen can survive in:
- Infected seed
- Infected crop debris
- Volunteer tomato and pepper plants
- Infected transplants
- Other contaminated plant material

Spread occurs primarily through:
- Rain splash
- Wind-driven rain
- Overhead irrigation
- Irrigation water
- Handling wet plants
- Contaminated tools and equipment
- Movement of infected seedlings

The bacteria enter tissues through natural openings and wounds.

Repeated wetting events can produce multiple infection cycles and allow disease to spread rapidly through a tomato crop.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Overhead irrigation
- Dense plantings
- Wet foliage during field operations
- Presence of infected plant material

Disease can spread particularly rapidly when plants remain wet for long periods.

## Detection

Tomato bacterial spot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop numerous small dark brown or black spots.
- Leaf lesions enlarge and merge under severe pressure.
- Immature fruit develops raised dark spots.
- Fruit lesions become rough or scabby.
- Disease increases following repeated rainfall or overhead irrigation.
- Symptoms occur across multiple plants in wet conditions.

Bacterial spot can resemble other tomato leaf-spot diseases. Diagnosis should therefore consider symptoms together with crop history, weather, and disease progression.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Bacterial spot can cause:
- Leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Stem and petiole lesions
- Fruit spotting and scarring
- Reduced fruit quality
- Reduced marketable yield
- Significant crop losses under severe disease pressure

Early severe infections can weaken plants and reduce their ability to support fruit development.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use certified or disease-free seed where available.
- Obtain healthy transplants from reputable nurseries.
- Avoid planting seedlings with visible bacterial-spot symptoms.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where suitable varieties are available.
- Avoid planting tomatoes immediately after severely affected tomato or pepper crops where practical.
- Maintain adequate spacing to improve airflow.

### During Growth
- Scout plants regularly for leaf and fruit symptoms.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Prefer irrigation methods that minimize foliage wetness.
- Avoid handling plants while foliage is wet.
- Sanitize tools and equipment used for plant handling and pruning.
- Remove severely infected plants or plant material where appropriate.
- Control volunteer tomato and pepper plants.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition and good crop vigor.
- Monitor disease development during warm, wet weather.

### After Harvest
- Remove or manage infected crop residues.
- Control volunteer solanaceous plants.
- Clean and sanitize tools and equipment.
- Avoid carrying infected plant material into subsequent crops.
- Practice crop rotation where practical.
- Review field history before planting another susceptible crop.

## Biological / Organic Management

Sanitation and moisture management are important nonchemical components of bacterial spot management.

Useful practices include:
- Using disease-free seed and transplants.
- Reducing foliage wetness.
- Removing infected plants.
- Crop rotation.
- Good field sanitation.
- Avoiding work in wet fields.

Some biological products may be incorporated into regional integrated disease-management programs, but effectiveness can vary with pathogen population, environmental conditions, and application timing.

Specific biological-control recommendations are not included in this initial record without current locally applicable evidence.

## Chemical Management

Preventive bactericide programs may be used when bacterial spot is economically important.

Copper-based products are commonly included in bacterial spot management programs, sometimes with other registered bactericidal products depending on local recommendations.

Chemical control is generally most useful when:
- Disease risk is identified early.
- Treatments begin before severe disease is established.
- Plant surfaces receive adequate protection.
- Chemical management is combined with cultural practices.

Reduced sensitivity to copper and other bactericides has been reported in some *Xanthomonas* populations. Resistance-management practices are therefore important.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato cultivars vary in susceptibility to bacterial spot.

Where available, cultivars with improved resistance or lower susceptibility can reduce disease severity.

Resistance should be verified for the specific cultivar and local *Xanthomonas* population because pathogen populations and disease pressure vary geographically.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato bacterial spot is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for small dark brown or black lesions.
2. Check immature fruit for raised or rough dark spots.
3. Review recent rainfall and irrigation conditions.
4. Stop unnecessary overhead irrigation.
5. Avoid handling plants while foliage is wet.
6. Remove severely infected plants where appropriate.
7. Sanitize tools and equipment.
8. Control volunteer tomato and pepper plants.
9. Monitor neighboring plants for new symptoms.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for confirmation and treatment decisions.
