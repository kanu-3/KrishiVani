# Tomato Early Blight

## Crop
Tomato

## Crop Aliases
- Tomato
- Tomato plant
- *Solanum lycopersicum*

## Disease
Early Blight

## Disease Aliases
- Tomato early blight
- Early blight of tomato
- Alternaria blight
- Target spot

## Scientific Name
*Alternaria solani*

## Common Names
- Early blight of tomato
- Early blight of potato
- Target spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen can infect tomato and other plants in the *Solanum* family, including:
- Potato
- Bell pepper / capsicum
- Eggplant
- Some wild *Solanum* species

## Symptoms
Early blight can affect leaves, stems, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small circular to angular brown spots initially appear on older leaves.
- Lesions may develop dark brown concentric rings, producing a characteristic target-like appearance.
- Lesions can merge as the disease progresses.
- Severely affected leaves may dry up and fall.
- Defoliation can expose fruit to excessive sunlight and cause sunscald.

### Stems
- Stem lesions are generally darker and more elongated than leaf lesions.
- Seedlings may develop large brown lesions near the soil line.
- Severe stem infection can cause stem breakage and plant death.

### Fruit
- Dark brown or black, sunken lesions may develop on fruit.
- Lesions may extend over part or most of the fruit.
- Infected tissue may develop dense black fungal spore masses.

## Disease Development and Spread
The pathogen can survive in crop residues and on contaminated seed.
Short-distance spread can occur when spores are moved by:
- Wind
- Rain
- Overhead irrigation water
- Contact between infected and healthy plants

## Favorable Conditions
Disease development is favored by:
- Warm weather
- Humid conditions
- Heavy dew
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
Poor airflow and prolonged moisture on foliage can increase disease development.

## Detection
Early blight can be suspected when:
- Older leaves develop circular or angular brown lesions.
- Lesions develop characteristic target-like concentric rings.
- Stem lesions are elongated and dark.
- Fruit develops dark, sunken lesions.
- Black fungal spore masses are visible on infected fruit.

## Disease Severity and Impact
Early blight can cause significant defoliation and reduce fruit yield and size.
Severe loss of foliage can expose fruit to sunscald.
The disease occurs in tomato-growing regions worldwide.

## Management
### Before Planting
- Use seed obtained from disease-free plants.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants and weeds belonging to the tomato family from nurseries and production areas.
- Inspect seedlings before transplanting and remove symptomatic plants.
- Select fields with good drainage and adequate airflow.

### During Growth
- Maintain sufficient spacing between plants to improve air circulation.
- Remove lower branches where appropriate to improve airflow around the plant base.
- Remove infected lower leaves as symptoms develop.
- Prefer irrigation at the base of the plant rather than overhead irrigation.
- If overhead irrigation is unavoidable, apply water early enough for foliage to dry before night.
- Control weeds that may act as sources of the pathogen.
- Maintain appropriate plant nutrition.

### After Harvest
- Remove crop residues from the field.
- Do not continuously plant tomato in the same field.
- Use crop rotation, preferably for 2–3 years, while avoiding susceptible crops in the same family.
- Avoid overlapping tomato crops, particularly where early blight has previously occurred.

## Cultural Practices
Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with disease-free seed and healthy seedlings.
- Maintaining adequate plant spacing.
- Improving airflow around plants.
- Minimizing prolonged leaf wetness.
- Avoiding unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Removing infected plant material.
- Controlling susceptible weeds.
- Removing crop residues after harvest.
- Practicing crop rotation.

## Chemical Management
The consulted source recommends protectant fungicides and mentions products containing:
- Mancozeb
- Chlorothalonil
- Copper products
It also mentions systemic fungicides such as strobilurins.
Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties
Complete resistance to early blight is not generally available, but tomato varieties can differ in their level of tolerance to the disease.

## Immediate Actions
When early blight is suspected:
1. Inspect older leaves for characteristic target-like lesions.
2. Check stems and fruit for additional lesions.
3. Remove severely infected plant material where appropriate.
4. Improve airflow around plants.
5. Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
6. Remove infected crop debris.
7. Monitor nearby plants for similar symptoms.
8. Consult a local agricultural expert or current extension recommendation for treatment decisions.

## Prevention
- Use disease-free seed and healthy seedlings.
- Maintain good airflow between plants.
- Avoid prolonged leaf wetness.
- Prefer irrigation at the plant base.
- Control weeds that may harbor the pathogen.
- Remove infected plant material.
- Remove crop residues after harvest.
- Practice crop rotation.
- Avoid overlapping susceptible tomato crops.
- Select varieties with improved tolerance where available.

## Related Crops
The pathogen can also affect:
- Potato
- Bell pepper / capsicum
- Eggplant
- Other susceptible *Solanum* hosts
