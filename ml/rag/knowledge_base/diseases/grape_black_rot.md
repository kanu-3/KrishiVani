# Grape Black Rot

## Crop
Grape

## Crop Aliases
- Grape
- Grape vine
- Grapevine
- *Vitis vinifera*

## Disease
Black Rot

## Disease Aliases
- Grape black rot
- Black rot of grape
- Grape black rot disease

## Scientific Name
*Guignardia bidwellii*

## Common Names
- Grape black rot
- Black rot of grape

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen primarily affects:
- Grape
- Grapevine
- Cultivated *Vitis* species
- Some wild grape species

## Symptoms

Black rot can affect leaves, shoots, tendrils, flowers, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small pale yellow to brown spots may initially develop on leaves.
- Lesions often enlarge into circular reddish-brown or tan spots.
- Lesions may develop dark margins.
- Small black fungal fruiting structures can form within older lesions.
- Numerous lesions can cause premature leaf yellowing and defoliation.

### Shoots and Tendrils
- Small reddish-brown to black lesions may develop on young shoots and tendrils.
- Lesions can become elongated and darken as they mature.
- Severe infections may weaken young shoots and tendrils.

### Flowers and Flower Clusters
- Infections can occur around flowering.
- Infected flower parts may darken and dry.
- Severe infection can cause flower or young fruit clusters to fail.

### Fruit
- Fruit infection is the most economically important symptom.
- Young berries may develop small pale or brown spots.
- Infected berries rapidly enlarge into brown, reddish-brown, or black rotted areas.
- Whole berries can shrivel and become hard black mummies.
- Small black fungal fruiting structures may appear on mature fruit lesions.
- Mummified berries can remain attached to clusters and become important sources of inoculum.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus survives between seasons primarily in:
- Mummified berries
- Infected rachises
- Infected tendrils
- Other diseased plant material

During wet weather, the fungus produces spores from infected tissues.

Spores spread through:
- Rain splash
- Wind-driven rain
- Water movement
- Contact with infected plant material

Primary infection commonly occurs during spring when susceptible tissues are exposed to spores.

Secondary infections can occur when favorable wet conditions continue.

Mummified berries left on vines are an important source of inoculum for future infections.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- Frequent rainfall
- High humidity
- Prolonged periods of leaf or fruit wetness
- Dense canopies
- Poor airflow
- Presence of mummified berries or infected plant debris

Extended wet periods around flowering and fruit development can substantially increase disease risk.

## Detection

Grape black rot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop circular brown or reddish-brown lesions.
- Black fungal fruiting structures appear within leaf lesions.
- Young shoots develop dark elongated lesions.
- Young berries develop brown spots that rapidly enlarge.
- Infected berries become black and shriveled.
- Black mummified berries remain attached to clusters.
- Disease increases following warm, wet weather.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Grape black rot can cause:
- Leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Shoot and tendril lesions
- Flower and cluster infection
- Fruit rot
- Extensive berry loss
- Reduced grape quality
- Significant yield loss under severe disease pressure

Fruit infection can become particularly damaging because infected berries may rapidly become mummified and unmarketable.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select healthy planting material from reliable sources.
- Choose grape cultivars with improved resistance or reduced susceptibility where available.
- Establish vines in sites with good air circulation and drainage.
- Use suitable trellising systems to improve canopy exposure and airflow.
- Avoid planting highly susceptible cultivars in locations with a history of severe disease where alternatives are available.

### During Growth
- Scout vines regularly for leaf, shoot, and fruit symptoms.
- Remove infected leaves, shoots, and tendrils where practical.
- Remove mummified berries from vines.
- Maintain good canopy airflow through pruning and training.
- Avoid excessive canopy density.
- Manage weeds that restrict airflow.
- Avoid irrigation practices that unnecessarily prolong foliage wetness.
- Monitor disease development during warm, wet periods.

### After Harvest
- Remove remaining mummified berries.
- Remove infected rachises and diseased plant material where practical.
- Maintain vineyard sanitation.
- Prune out heavily infected tissues where appropriate.
- Reduce overwintering sources of inoculum before the following season.

Removing mummified fruit is particularly important because these tissues can produce spores during wet weather and contribute to disease development in the following season.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Removing mummified berries.
- Removing infected plant material.
- Improving canopy airflow.
- Proper vine training and pruning.
- Avoiding excessive canopy density.
- Managing weeds.
- Minimizing prolonged foliage wetness.
- Starting with healthy planting material.
- Selecting less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Maintaining vineyard sanitation.

## Biological / Organic Management

Sanitation and canopy management are important nonchemical components of grape black rot management.

Removing mummified berries and infected plant tissues can substantially reduce the amount of fungal inoculum available for future infections.

Improving airflow and reducing prolonged wetness can also reduce favorable conditions for disease development.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be required where grape black rot is a recurring or economically important disease.

Preventive applications are generally more effective than attempting to control severe established infection.

Chemical-management decisions should consider:
- Cultivar susceptibility
- Vineyard disease history
- Weather conditions
- Crop growth stage
- Disease severity
- Current local agricultural recommendations

Fungicide applications are particularly important during susceptible growth stages when wet conditions are expected.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Products should therefore be selected and rotated according to current resistance-management guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Grape cultivars vary in susceptibility to black rot.

Where available, cultivars with improved resistance or reduced susceptibility can help reduce disease pressure.

Cultivar selection should consider:
- Local disease pressure
- Climate
- Production system
- Fruit-use requirements
- Current resistance information

Resistance should be verified for the specific cultivar and local growing conditions.

## Immediate Actions

When grape black rot is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for circular brown or reddish-brown lesions.
2. Check leaves for black fungal fruiting structures.
3. Inspect young shoots and tendrils for dark lesions.
4. Check clusters for brown or black rotting berries.
5. Remove black mummified berries where practical.
6. Remove severely infected plant material.
7. Improve canopy airflow through appropriate pruning and training.
8. Avoid unnecessary prolonged foliage wetness.
9. Monitor disease progression after warm, wet weather.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use healthy planting material.
- Select less susceptible grape cultivars where available.
- Remove mummified berries from vines.
- Remove infected plant material.
- Maintain good canopy airflow.
- Use appropriate vine training and pruning.
- Avoid excessive canopy density.
- Reduce prolonged foliage wetness.
- Monitor vines regularly during wet weather.
- Use preventive disease-management programs when justified by local risk.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Maintain vineyard sanitation.
