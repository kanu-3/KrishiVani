# Apple Scab

## Crop
Apple

## Crop Aliases
- Apple
- Apple tree
- *Malus domestica*

## Disease
Apple Scab

## Disease Aliases
- Apple scab
- Apple black scab
- Scab of apple

## Scientific Name
*Venturia inaequalis*

## Common Names
- Apple scab
- Apple black scab

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen primarily affects apple and crabapple species (*Malus*), including cultivated and ornamental apples.

## Symptoms

Apple scab can affect leaves, flowers, fruit, and young shoots.

### Leaves
- Small pale green, olive, or brown lesions may appear on young leaves.
- Lesions commonly develop an olive-green to dark velvety appearance as the fungus produces spores.
- Older lesions may become brown and necrotic.
- Lesions can enlarge and merge when disease pressure is high.
- Severely affected leaves may become distorted and fall prematurely.

### Flowers
- Infection can occur on flower parts during wet spring weather.
- Severe infection may contribute to poor fruit set or loss of infected flowers.

### Fruit
- Young fruit may develop olive, brown, or black scabby lesions.
- Early infections can cause fruit to become distorted or cracked as it enlarges.
- Older lesions become corky and may remain visible on mature fruit.
- Severe fruit infection can reduce marketability and quality.

### Shoots
- Some apple and crabapple tissues can develop scab lesions.
- Shoot infection is generally less important than infection of leaves and fruit.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus survives primarily in infected fallen leaves.

During spring, overwintering structures in fallen leaves release spores that can infect newly emerging leaves and developing fruit during wet periods.

Secondary spores can then be produced on infected plant tissue and spread the disease repeatedly during the growing season.

Spread occurs primarily through:
- Rain splash
- Wind-driven rain
- Air movement carrying spores
- Contact with wet infected plant material

Apple scab development is strongly associated with periods when susceptible tissue remains wet for sufficient time for infection.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Cool to moderate temperatures
- Frequent spring rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- High humidity
- Dense foliage with poor airflow

Initial infection is particularly important during spring, when young leaves and fruit are highly susceptible.

## Detection

Apple scab can be suspected when:
- Young leaves develop olive-green or brown lesions.
- Lesions have a velvety fungal appearance.
- Lesions become darker and more necrotic with age.
- Fruit develops dark scabby or corky lesions.
- Severely affected leaves become distorted or drop prematurely.
- Symptoms repeatedly appear following wet spring weather.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Apple scab can cause:
- Premature leaf drop
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Poor fruit development
- Fruit cracking and distortion
- Reduced fruit quality
- Significant loss of marketable fruit under severe disease pressure

Severe primary infections early in the season can make disease management considerably more difficult later in the season.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select apple cultivars with improved resistance or tolerance to apple scab where suitable varieties are available.
- Establish orchards in sites with good air circulation and drainage.
- Obtain healthy planting material from reliable sources.
- Avoid selecting planting material with obvious disease symptoms.

### During Growth
- Monitor young leaves and developing fruit regularly, particularly during wet spring weather.
- Maintain adequate spacing and pruning to improve airflow.
- Avoid irrigation practices that unnecessarily prolong leaf wetness.
- Monitor weather conditions and disease-development periods.
- Use an integrated disease-management program where scab has historically been important.

### After Harvest
- Remove or accelerate decomposition of fallen infected leaves to reduce overwintering inoculum.
- Maintain orchard sanitation.
- Remove heavily diseased plant material where practical.
- Plan disease management for the following spring because the pathogen survives in fallen leaves.

Removing fallen leaves can reduce the amount of primary inoculum available for spring infection.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Removing fallen leaves.
- Promoting rapid decomposition of leaf litter.
- Maintaining good airflow through pruning.
- Avoiding prolonged foliage wetness.
- Monitoring the orchard during wet spring periods.
- Selecting resistant or less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Maintaining good orchard sanitation.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural sanitation is an important nonchemical component of apple scab management.

Removal or accelerated decomposition of fallen leaves can reduce the overwintering fungal inoculum. In some production systems, materials used to accelerate leaf decomposition may be incorporated into an integrated management program.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Preventive fungicide programs are commonly used where apple scab poses a significant risk.

Management is generally most effective when treatments are timed according to crop development, weather conditions, infection risk, and local disease-warning systems rather than waiting for extensive symptoms.

Some fungicides used for scab management have single-site modes of action, and repeated use of the same mode of action can select for fungicide-resistant pathogen populations. Fungicides should therefore be selected and rotated according to current local recommendations and resistance-management guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Apple cultivars differ substantially in susceptibility to apple scab.

Some cultivars have been bred or selected for increased resistance to scab. Resistant or less susceptible cultivars can substantially reduce disease pressure and the need for chemical intervention in suitable production systems.

## Immediate Actions

When apple scab is suspected:

1. Inspect young leaves for olive-green or brown lesions.
2. Check developing fruit for scabby or corky lesions.
3. Examine the orchard for widespread symptoms following wet weather.
4. Remove heavily infected fallen leaves where practical.
5. Improve airflow through appropriate pruning.
6. Avoid unnecessary prolonged leaf wetness.
7. Monitor newly emerging leaves and fruit for additional symptoms.
8. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for current treatment recommendations.

## Prevention

- Select scab-resistant or less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Remove fallen infected leaves.
- Encourage rapid decomposition of leaf litter.
- Maintain good orchard airflow.
- Minimize unnecessary foliage wetness.
- Monitor weather and infection-risk periods.
- Start preventive disease management early in spring where scab is historically important.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide regulations.

## Related Crops

The pathogen primarily affects:
- Apple
- Crabapple
- Other susceptible *Malus* species
