## Hosts / Related Crops
Tomato mosaic virus can infect a broad range of plants, including:
- Tomato
- Tobacco
- Pepper
- Other susceptible solanaceous plants
- Some ornamental and weed hosts

## Symptoms

Tomato mosaic virus can affect leaves, stems, flowers, and fruit and can reduce overall plant growth.

### Leaves
- Leaves may develop light and dark green mottling or mosaic patterns.
- Areas of normal and abnormal green tissue may occur irregularly across the leaf.
- Young leaves may become distorted or narrowed.
- Leaves may develop a fern-like, shoestring, or filamentous appearance in some infections.
- Leaf surfaces can become wrinkled or puckered.
- Severe infection can reduce normal leaf expansion.

### Stems and Plant Growth
- Plants may become stunted.
- Internodes may be shortened.
- Overall plant vigor can decline.
- Severe infection in young plants can cause substantial growth reduction.

### Flowers
- Flower development may become abnormal.
- Flower abortion can occur under severe infection.
- Reduced flower development can contribute to lower fruit set.

### Fruit
- Fruit may develop uneven ripening.
- Light and dark green mottling may remain visible on immature fruit.
- Fruit may be smaller than normal.
- Shape and internal development can be abnormal.
- Some infected fruit can show brown or necrotic internal tissue.
- Fruit quality and marketability may be reduced.

## Disease Development and Spread

Tomato mosaic virus is highly contagious and is primarily spread mechanically rather than by an insect vector.

The virus can spread through:
- Contact between infected and healthy plants
- Contaminated hands
- Clothing
- Tools and equipment
- Pruning and handling activities
- Contaminated transplants
- Infected seed
- Plant debris
- Contact with infected roots or sap

The virus is highly stable and can survive for extended periods in dried plant material and on contaminated surfaces.

Seeds can also carry the virus on their surface or, depending on the seed source and infection conditions, internally.

Because even small amounts of infected plant sap can transmit the virus, routine greenhouse, nursery, and field handling practices can contribute substantially to spread.

## Favorable Conditions

Virus spread is favored by:
- Frequent handling of plants.
- Pruning and transplanting operations.
- Mechanical injury.
- Contaminated tools and equipment.
- Close contact between plants.
- Infected seed or transplants.
- Presence of infected plant debris.
- Continuous production of susceptible tomato plants.

Environmental conditions primarily influence plant growth and symptom expression rather than virus transmission itself.

## Detection

Tomato mosaic virus can be suspected when:
- Leaves show irregular light and dark green mosaic patterns.
- Young leaves are distorted, narrowed, or puckered.
- Plants show unexplained stunting.
- Fruit develops uneven coloration or abnormal ripening.
- Similar symptoms occur on multiple plants following handling or transplanting operations.
- Symptoms occur without the typical insect-vector pattern associated with viruses such as tomato yellow leaf curl virus.

Visual symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish ToMV from all other tomato viruses. Laboratory testing may be required for definitive identification.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Tomato mosaic virus can cause:
- Leaf mottling
- Leaf distortion
- Plant stunting
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Flower abnormalities
- Poor fruit set
- Uneven fruit ripening
- Reduced fruit size and quality
- Reduced yield under severe infection

Early infection can have a greater effect on plant growth and yield than infection occurring late in the crop cycle.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use certified virus-free seed and healthy transplants.
- Obtain seed and planting material from reliable suppliers.
- Avoid using seed saved from plants with suspected viral symptoms.
- Sanitize propagation areas, trays, benches, and tools.
- Select resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Avoid introducing unknown plant material into established tomato production systems.

### During Growth
- Inspect plants regularly for mosaic symptoms and abnormal growth.
- Wash hands before and after handling plants.
- Sanitize pruning knives, scissors, stakes, tools, and equipment.
- Remove infected plants where practical and dispose of them without spreading sap to healthy plants.
- Avoid handling healthy plants immediately after infected plants.
- Avoid smoking or handling tobacco products around tomato plants when appropriate because tobacco materials can carry related tobamoviruses.
- Control volunteer tomato and susceptible weed hosts.
- Avoid unnecessary mechanical injury.
- Maintain good crop sanitation.

### After Harvest
- Remove and destroy infected crop residues.
- Sanitize greenhouse structures, stakes, tools, benches, trays, and other reusable equipment.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants.
- Avoid carrying infected plant material into the next crop.
- Review seed and transplant sources before the next production cycle.
- Maintain strict sanitation between successive tomato crops.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with certified or tested virus-free seed.
- Using healthy transplants.
- Washing hands before plant handling.
- Sanitizing tools and equipment.
- Removing infected plants promptly.
- Avoiding unnecessary mechanical injury.
- Avoiding cross-contamination between infected and healthy plants.
- Controlling volunteer tomato and weed hosts.
- Maintaining greenhouse and nursery sanitation.
- Removing infected crop residues after harvest.

Because ToMV is mechanically transmitted and highly stable, sanitation and prevention of plant-to-plant contact are particularly important.

## Biological / Organic Management

There is no established biological treatment that cures a tomato plant after tomato mosaic virus infection has become established.

Important nonchemical management practices include:
- Virus-free planting material.
- Resistant or tolerant cultivars.
- Sanitation.
- Removal of infected plants.
- Prevention of mechanical transmission.
- Control of volunteer and alternative host plants.

Biological-control agents used for insect pests do not cure ToMV because the principal transmission pathway is mechanical rather than insect-mediated.

## Chemical Management

There is no chemical treatment that cures tomato mosaic virus-infected plants.

Fungicides and conventional insecticides do not eliminate ToMV from infected plants.

Chemical inputs should therefore not be presented as a treatment for the virus itself.

Where insect or other pest management is required for separate problems, products should be selected according to the specific pest diagnosis and current local agricultural guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato cultivars with resistance or tolerance to tomato mosaic virus are available.

Resistance has historically been associated with specific host resistance genes, and resistant varieties can substantially reduce symptom development and crop losses.

However:
- Resistance effectiveness can depend on the virus strain.
- Resistant cultivars may not provide protection against every related tobamovirus.
- Seed suppliers' current resistance designations should be verified before planting.

Where ToMV is a recurring problem, resistant cultivars should be combined with strict sanitation because resistant varieties are not a substitute for preventing mechanical virus transmission.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato mosaic virus is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for light and dark green mosaic patterns.
2. Check young leaves for distortion, puckering, or narrowing.
3. Assess plants for stunting or shortened internodes.
4. Examine fruit for uneven coloring or abnormal ripening.
5. Isolate or mark suspicious plants where practical.
6. Avoid handling healthy plants immediately after suspicious plants.
7. Wash hands and sanitize tools and equipment.
8. Remove infected plants where appropriate.
9. Avoid moving potentially infected plant material between production areas.
10. Obtain laboratory confirmation when ToMV or another tobamovirus needs to be distinguished.

## Prevention

- Use certified or tested virus-free seed and transplants.
- Select ToMV-resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Sanitize tools and equipment regularly.
- Wash hands before handling plants.
- Avoid unnecessary plant injury.
- Remove infected plants promptly.
- Control volunteer tomato plants.
- Control susceptible weed hosts.
- Avoid moving infected plant material between fields or greenhouse sections.
- Sanitize propagation equipment and production structures.
- Remove crop residue after harvest.
- Maintain strict nursery and greenhouse hygiene.
- Do not rely on insecticide use as a treatment for ToMV.

Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) is a tobamovirus that is highly stable and is transmitted primarily through mechanical contact, contaminated tools, handling, infected planting material, and seed. Characteristic symptoms include light and dark green mosaic patterns, leaf distortion, stunting, and abnormal fruit development.

ToMV should be kept distinct from Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus, which is a begomovirus primarily transmitted by whiteflies. The two diseases have different transmission pathways and management priorities.

There is no chemical cure for established ToMV infection. Prevention therefore depends heavily on clean planting material, resistant cultivars where available, strict sanitation, and prevention of mechanical transmission.
