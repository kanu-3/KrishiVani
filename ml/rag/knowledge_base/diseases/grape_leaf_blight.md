## Symptoms

Leaf blight primarily affects grape leaves, although severe infections can contribute to broader vine decline through premature defoliation.

### Leaves
- Small irregular, angular, or roughly circular brown spots may develop on leaves.
- Lesions can enlarge and become dark brown.
- Older lesions may develop darker margins.
- Affected areas may become necrotic.
- Severe infections can cause multiple lesions to merge.
- Large portions of leaves may become damaged or dead.
- Heavily infected leaves may fall prematurely.

### Lower Leaves
- Symptoms may become more noticeable on older or lower leaves as disease develops.
- Progressive infection can move through the canopy when conditions remain favorable.

### Shoots and Fruit
- The disease is primarily a foliar disease.
- Severe defoliation can indirectly affect shoot development and fruit maturation by reducing the photosynthetic leaf area.
- Fruit symptoms are not generally the primary diagnostic feature of this disease.

## Disease Development and Spread

The pathogen produces spores on infected grape tissues.

Spores can spread through:
- Rain splash
- Wind-driven rain
- Water movement
- Contact with infected foliage

Infection is favored when susceptible leaves remain wet for sufficient periods.

Disease can develop through repeated infection cycles when humid and wet conditions persist.

Infected leaves and other plant debris can contribute to pathogen survival between growing seasons.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm and humid weather
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Dense grape canopies
- Poor air circulation
- Abundant infected leaf material

Conditions that prevent rapid drying of foliage can increase disease development.

## Detection

Grape leaf blight can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop irregular or angular brown spots.
- Lesions become dark brown and necrotic.
- Multiple lesions merge across the leaf surface.
- Older leaves show increasing numbers of spots.
- Premature defoliation occurs under severe disease pressure.
- Symptoms increase after warm, wet weather.

The characteristic leaf spots should be distinguished from other grape leaf-spot diseases because several fungal pathogens can produce similar symptoms.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Grape leaf blight generally causes limited damage in well-managed vineyards, but severe infections can cause:
- Extensive leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Reduced vine vigor
- Reduced accumulation of carbohydrates in fruit
- Reduced fruit quality
- Reduced sugar accumulation under severe disease pressure

Severe and repeated defoliation can interfere with normal vine development and fruit maturation. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Start with healthy planting material.
- Select grape cultivars adapted to the local environment.
- Consider cultivar susceptibility where reliable local information is available.
- Establish vineyards with adequate spacing for air circulation.
- Use trellising and training systems that allow the canopy to dry quickly.

### During Growth
- Scout grape leaves regularly for early symptoms.
- Maintain good canopy airflow through pruning and shoot positioning.
- Remove excessively dense foliage where appropriate.
- Control weeds that restrict airflow around vines.
- Maintain good vine vigor.
- Avoid irrigation practices that unnecessarily prolong leaf wetness.
- Remove severely infected foliage where practical.
- Monitor disease development during warm and wet periods.

Good airflow and rapid drying of plant tissues are important components of management for grape leaf-spot diseases. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### After Harvest
- Remove or manage heavily infected fallen foliage where practical.
- Maintain vineyard sanitation.
- Review canopy-management practices before the following season.
- Monitor fields with a history of recurring leaf-spot disease.
- Avoid leaving heavily infected plant material as an unnecessary inoculum source.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Maintaining good vine vigor.
- Improving air circulation through pruning and shoot positioning.
- Maintaining suitable vine spacing.
- Managing weeds.
- Reducing dense canopy growth.
- Minimizing prolonged foliage wetness.
- Removing severely infected plant material.
- Maintaining vineyard sanitation.
- Monitoring vines regularly.

## Chemical Management

Leaf spot diseases of grape are often controlled incidentally by fungicide programs used for more economically important grape diseases. Well-managed vineyards may experience little economic impact from Pseudocercospora leaf spot. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Where disease becomes economically important, fungicides may be considered based on:
- Disease history
- Disease severity
- Weather conditions
- Crop growth stage
- Cultivar susceptibility
- Current local agricultural recommendations

Protective fungicide programs are generally more useful when disease is managed early rather than after severe defoliation has occurred.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Products should therefore be selected and rotated according to current resistance-management guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Grape cultivars can differ in their susceptibility to leaf-spot diseases.

Where reliable local information is available, less susceptible cultivars may be preferred in areas with recurring disease pressure.

Specific resistance claims should be verified for the cultivar and local growing conditions.

## Immediate Actions

When grape leaf blight is suspected:

1. Inspect older and lower leaves for irregular brown lesions.
2. Check whether lesions are increasing after warm, wet weather.
3. Inspect the rest of the canopy for similar symptoms.
4. Improve airflow through appropriate pruning and shoot positioning.
5. Reduce excessive canopy density where practical.
6. Remove severely infected foliage where appropriate.
7. Avoid unnecessary prolonged leaf wetness.
8. Monitor the rate of disease progression.
9. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert if symptoms are difficult to distinguish from other grape leaf diseases.

## Prevention

- Start with healthy planting material.
- Select suitable grape cultivars.
- Maintain good canopy airflow.
- Use appropriate pruning and shoot positioning.
- Avoid excessive canopy density.
- Control weeds around vines.
- Minimize prolonged leaf wetness.
- Maintain good vine vigor.
- Remove heavily infected plant material where practical.
- Monitor vines regularly during warm and wet periods.
- Use chemical control only when justified by local disease risk.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
