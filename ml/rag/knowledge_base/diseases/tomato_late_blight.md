## Symptoms

Late blight can affect leaves, stems, petioles, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small water-soaked or pale green lesions may initially appear on leaves.
- Lesions enlarge rapidly and become irregular dark green, olive, brown, or black blotches.
- Lesions are generally not restricted by major leaf veins.
- A green-gray margin may surround actively expanding lesions.
- Under cool and humid conditions, thin white fungal-like growth may develop around lesions, particularly on the underside of leaves.
- Multiple lesions can merge and destroy large areas of foliage.
- Severely infected leaves may turn brown, shrivel, and collapse rapidly.

### Stems and Petioles
- Firm dark brown to black lesions may develop on stems and petioles.
- Lesions can enlarge and cause affected tissues to weaken.
- Severe stem infection can cause shoots to wilt and collapse.
- White sporulation may develop on infected stem tissue during humid conditions.

### Fruit
- Green and ripe tomatoes can both become infected.
- Firm dark brown or olive-colored lesions may initially develop on fruit.
- Lesions enlarge and can cover a substantial portion of the fruit.
- Infected areas may become sunken or wrinkled.
- Secondary bacteria can invade infected tissue and cause soft rot.
- Fruit may become unmarketable and decay rapidly.

## Disease Development and Spread

*Phytophthora infestans* is an oomycete, commonly called a water mold.

The pathogen can be introduced into tomato fields through:
- Infected tomato transplants
- Infected potato plants or tubers
- Windblown spores
- Infected volunteer plants
- Other infected plant material

Spores can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Airborne movement
- Water movement
- Handling of infected plant material

Under cool and wet conditions, infected tissue can produce large numbers of sporangia. These structures can become airborne and initiate new infections over substantial areas.

Disease can progress extremely rapidly when favorable conditions persist.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Cool temperatures
- High relative humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Heavy dew
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Wet foliage
- Dense canopies
- Presence of infected tomato or potato plants

Late blight is particularly favored by cool, damp conditions. Prolonged hot and dry conditions can slow disease development. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## Detection

Tomato late blight can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop large irregular water-soaked, olive, brown, or dark lesions.
- Lesions are not confined by major leaf veins.
- White fungal-like growth appears on infected tissue during humid weather.
- Stems and petioles develop firm dark lesions.
- Green or ripe fruit develops firm dark brown or olive lesions.
- Disease progresses rapidly across the crop following cool, wet weather.

Late blight can be confused with other tomato leaf diseases, so lesion structure, weather conditions, disease progression, and nearby infected potato or tomato plants should all be considered.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Tomato late blight can cause:
- Rapid foliar destruction
- Severe defoliation
- Stem and petiole collapse
- Fruit rot
- Reduced fruit quality
- Reduced yield
- Complete crop failure under severe conditions

The disease can spread rapidly enough to destroy a field within a short period when conditions are highly favorable. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use healthy tomato transplants from reputable nurseries.
- Inspect transplants for late-blight symptoms before planting.
- Select cultivars with improved late-blight resistance where available.
- Avoid planting tomatoes immediately adjacent to known infected potato or tomato crops where practical.
- Use suitable spacing to improve airflow.
- Select fields with good sunlight and drainage.

### During Growth
- Scout tomato plants frequently, especially during cool and wet weather.
- Remove diseased plant tissue promptly where practical.
- Remove severely infected plants when necessary to reduce inoculum.
- Control volunteer potato and tomato plants.
- Avoid overhead irrigation where possible.
- Water in the morning when irrigation is necessary so foliage can dry quickly.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing and airflow.
- Monitor regional late-blight forecasts where available.
- Begin preventive disease management when disease risk is high.

### After Harvest
- Remove infected tomato plants and fruit from the production area.
- Do not leave severely infected plants in piles where living tissue can support pathogen survival.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants.
- Destroy or appropriately manage infected potato cull piles nearby.
- Maintain field sanitation.
- Review disease history before the next planting season.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with healthy transplants.
- Selecting resistant or less susceptible cultivars.
- Avoiding prolonged foliage wetness.
- Using drip or root-zone irrigation where practical.
- Watering early enough for foliage to dry.
- Maintaining adequate plant spacing.
- Improving airflow through staking, caging, and pruning where appropriate.
- Removing infected plants and plant debris.
- Controlling volunteer potato and tomato hosts.
- Monitoring disease forecasts and field conditions.

## Biological / Organic Management

There is no established biological treatment that reliably cures a tomato plant after severe late-blight infection is established.

Important nonchemical approaches include:
- Healthy transplant selection.
- Resistant or tolerant cultivars.
- Early detection.
- Removal of infected plants.
- Field sanitation.
- Reduction of leaf wetness.
- Good canopy airflow.

Organic production systems may use approved protectant products, but their effectiveness depends strongly on early and preventive application.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides are commonly used for late-blight management where disease risk is significant.

Because the pathogen can spread very rapidly, fungicides are generally most effective when applied preventively or at the first signs of disease rather than after extensive infection has developed. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Chemical management should consider:
- Disease risk.
- Weather conditions.
- Crop growth stage.
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Regional disease forecasts.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

*P. infestans* is an oomycete, so products intended for water-mold diseases should be selected according to current local recommendations.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can select for resistant pathogen populations. Fungicide groups should therefore be rotated or combined according to current resistance-management guidance. ([extension.psu.edu](https://extension.psu.edu/2026-late-blight-updates?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato cultivars differ in their susceptibility to late blight.

Some cultivars possess partial resistance or improved tolerance and can reduce disease development.

Resistance effectiveness may vary with the *P. infestans* population or clonal lineage present in a region. Even resistant cultivars may develop symptoms when disease pressure and weather conditions are highly favorable. ([extension.umn.edu](https://extension.umn.edu/disease-management/late-blight?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Specific resistance claims should be verified for the cultivar and local disease environment.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato late blight is suspected:

1. Inspect leaves for large irregular water-soaked or dark lesions.
2. Check whether lesions are unrestricted by major leaf veins.
3. Examine the underside of leaves during humid conditions for white sporulation.
4. Inspect stems and petioles for firm dark lesions.
5. Check green and ripe fruit for dark brown or olive lesions.
6. Review recent weather conditions for cool, wet periods.
7. Inspect nearby tomato and potato plants for symptoms.
8. Remove infected tissue or severely infected plants where appropriate.
9. Avoid overhead irrigation.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert promptly because late blight can spread rapidly.

## Prevention

- Use healthy tomato transplants.
- Inspect transplants before planting.
- Select late-blight-resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
- Monitor local late-blight forecasts.
- Scout plants frequently during cool, wet weather.
- Avoid prolonged leaf wetness.
- Prefer drip or root-zone irrigation.
- Maintain adequate spacing and airflow.
- Remove diseased plants and fruit.
- Control volunteer tomato and potato plants.
- Manage infected potato cull piles.
- Begin fungicide protection based on disease risk rather than waiting for severe symptoms.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
