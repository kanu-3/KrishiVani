# Potato Early Blight

## Crop
Potato

## Crop Aliases
- Potato
- Irish potato
- *Solanum tuberosum*

## Disease
Early Blight

## Disease Aliases
- Potato early blight
- Early blight of potato
- Alternaria leaf spot
- Target spot

## Scientific Name
*Alternaria solani*

## Common Names
- Potato early blight
- Early blight
- Alternaria leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen can affect several plants in the *Solanum* family, including:
- Potato
- Tomato
- Eggplant
- Other susceptible *Solanum* species

## Symptoms

Early blight can affect leaves, stems, and tubers.

### Leaves
- Small brown to dark-brown spots may initially develop on older leaves.
- Lesions often develop concentric rings, producing a characteristic target-like appearance.
- Lesions may be surrounded by yellow or chlorotic tissue.
- Multiple lesions can enlarge and merge.
- Severe infection can cause extensive leaf necrosis.
- Older leaves are generally affected first, with symptoms progressing upward under favorable conditions.
- Severe defoliation can expose tubers to increased environmental stress.

### Stems
- Dark brown to black elongated lesions may develop on stems and petioles.
- Severe stem infection can weaken affected tissues.
- Young plants may be more severely affected when environmental stress is also present.

### Tubers
- Tubers can develop shallow, dark, irregularly shaped lesions.
- Infected areas may become slightly sunken.
- Brown to black necrotic tissue can develop beneath the tuber surface.
- Tuber lesions may provide entry points for secondary organisms and storage rots.
- Severe infection can reduce tuber quality and marketability.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus survives in:
- Infected crop residue
- Infected potato tubers
- Volunteer potato plants
- Other susceptible host material

Spores are produced on infected tissues and can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Irrigation water
- Contact with infected plant material

Disease develops through repeated infection cycles during the growing season.

Early blight is often more severe in older or stressed plants. Nutrient deficiencies, drought stress, and other conditions that reduce plant vigor can increase susceptibility.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Plant stress
- Dense foliage
- Poor airflow
- Nutrient deficiencies
- Older foliage

Alternaria infection can increase when plants experience drought or other stress conditions.

## Detection

Potato early blight can be suspected when:
- Older leaves develop small brown spots.
- Lesions develop characteristic concentric rings.
- Lesions are surrounded by yellowing or chlorotic tissue.
- Disease progresses from older leaves toward newer foliage.
- Stems develop dark elongated lesions.
- Tubers develop shallow dark lesions.
- Symptoms increase under warm, humid conditions.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Early blight can cause:
- Premature leaf senescence
- Extensive defoliation
- Reduced photosynthesis
- Reduced tuber development
- Reduced tuber size
- Reduced yield
- Reduced tuber quality
- Increased storage losses when tubers are infected

Early disease occurring before or during tuber bulking can have a greater effect on yield than late-season infection.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use certified healthy seed potatoes.
- Select cultivars with improved early-blight resistance or tolerance where available.
- Avoid planting visibly diseased seed tubers.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Avoid planting potatoes repeatedly in the same field when disease pressure is high.
- Maintain adequate soil fertility based on soil testing.

### During Growth
- Scout fields regularly, especially older lower leaves.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition.
- Avoid drought stress through appropriate irrigation.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing and airflow.
- Avoid unnecessary prolonged foliage wetness.
- Remove volunteer potato plants.
- Manage weeds and other susceptible hosts.
- Monitor disease development during warm and wet weather.
- Use preventive disease-management programs where justified.

### After Harvest
- Remove or manage infected crop residue.
- Avoid leaving cull potatoes in fields.
- Control volunteer potatoes.
- Practice crop rotation where practical.
- Store harvested tubers under conditions that reduce secondary rotting.
- Review field history before the following potato crop.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural practices are the main nonchemical component of early-blight management.

Useful practices include:
- Healthy seed selection.
- Crop rotation.
- Residue management.
- Maintaining plant vigor.
- Adequate irrigation.
- Reduced leaf wetness.
- Regular scouting.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides are commonly used to manage potato early blight when disease pressure is significant.

Preventive or early applications are generally more effective than attempting to control severe established disease.

Treatment decisions should consider:
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Disease history.
- Weather conditions.
- Disease severity.
- Crop growth stage.
- Expected yield.
- Current local recommendations.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Fungicide programs should therefore rotate modes of action according to current resistance-management guidance.

**Important:** Chemical recommendations, permitted products, formulations, application rates, resistance-management requirements, pre-harvest intervals, and regulations vary by country and can change over time. ## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Potato cultivars vary in susceptibility to early blight.

Cultivars with improved tolerance or resistance can reduce disease severity and may reduce dependence on fungicide applications.

Resistance should be verified for the specific potato cultivar and local disease environment.

## Immediate Actions

When potato early blight is suspected:

1. Inspect older leaves for brown target-like lesions.
2. Check whether lesions are surrounded by yellow tissue.
3. Monitor progression from lower to upper foliage.
4. Inspect stems and petioles for dark lesions.
5. Check tubers for shallow dark lesions after harvest or sampling.
6. Assess recent weather and plant stress conditions.
7. Maintain adequate irrigation and balanced nutrition.
8. Remove volunteer potatoes and susceptible weeds.
9. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use certified disease-free seed potatoes.
- Select less susceptible cultivars where available.
- Practice crop rotation.
- Remove volunteer potatoes.
- Manage infected crop residue.
- Maintain balanced nutrition.
- Avoid drought stress.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing and airflow.
- Reduce prolonged foliage wetness.
- Monitor older leaves regularly.
- Start disease management early when risk is high.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
