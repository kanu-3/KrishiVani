# Tomato Target Spot

Tomato

Target Spot

- Tomato target spot

## Scientific Name
*Corynespora cassiicola*

## Common Names
- Target spot
- Tomato target spot
- Corynespora leaf spot
- Target leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen has a broad host range and can infect numerous cultivated and wild plants.

Important hosts can include:
- Tomato
- Cucumber
- Melon
- Papaya
- Soybean
- Bean
- Cotton
- Other susceptible plant species

## Symptoms

Target spot can affect leaves, stems, petioles, and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small brown spots may initially appear on older or lower leaves.
- Lesions gradually enlarge and develop concentric rings.
- Larger lesions can develop a characteristic target-like appearance.
- Lesion centers may become tan, gray, or brown as tissue dies.
- A yellow halo may sometimes develop around older lesions.
- Lesions can merge when disease pressure is high.
- Severe infection can cause large areas of leaf tissue to become necrotic.
- Heavily infected leaves may yellow and drop prematurely.

### Stems and Petioles
- Brown to dark lesions may develop on stems and petioles.
- Lesions can become elongated or irregular.
- Severe lesions may weaken affected tissues.

### Fruit
- Fruit lesions may begin as small dark spots.
- Lesions can enlarge and become slightly sunken.
- Brown to dark concentric rings may develop around fruit lesions.
- Fruit lesions may become leathery or corky.
- Severe infection can reduce fruit marketability.
- Fruit infection is generally more important as fruit matures and when humidity remains high.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus can survive in:
- Infected crop residue
- Infected plant material
- Soil-associated plant debris
- Volunteer plants
- Other susceptible hosts

Spores can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Irrigation water
- Contact with infected plant tissue

The pathogen can infect leaves and fruit when moisture remains on plant surfaces.

Repeated periods of wetness can result in multiple infection cycles and rapid disease development.

Because the pathogen has a broad host range, weeds and other crops may contribute to pathogen survival between tomato crops.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Dense foliage
- Poor air circulation
- Heavy canopy growth
- Presence of infected plant debris

Target spot is especially favored by warm, humid conditions and extended periods of leaf wetness.

## Detection

Tomato target spot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop brown spots that enlarge into target-like lesions.
- Lesions develop concentric rings.
- Disease begins in older or lower foliage and progresses upward.
- Leaves become yellow and drop under severe infection.
- Fruit develops dark, slightly sunken lesions with concentric rings.
- Disease increases following warm, wet weather.

Target spot can resemble early blight and other tomato leaf-spot diseases. The exact lesion appearance, disease distribution, fruit symptoms, and environmental conditions should therefore be considered together. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Disease Severity and Impact

Target spot can cause:
- Extensive leaf spotting
- Premature defoliation
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Reduced plant vigor
- Fruit spotting and scarring
- Reduced marketable yield
- Reduced fruit quality
- Yield loss when infection is severe or occurs early

Severe defoliation can reduce the plant's ability to support fruit development and ripening.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use healthy tomato seed and transplants.
- Select cultivars with improved tolerance where suitable varieties are available.
- Avoid planting in fields with heavy disease residue where practical.
- Use suitable spacing to improve airflow.
- Establish tomatoes in well-drained sites.
- Remove volunteer tomato plants and weeds that may maintain the pathogen.

### During Growth
- Scout lower leaves regularly for early symptoms.
- Remove severely infected leaves where practical.
- Maintain good airflow through staking, trellising, or appropriate pruning.
- Avoid unnecessary overhead irrigation.
- Use drip or base irrigation where practical.
- Apply mulch to reduce soil and crop-residue splash.
- Remove weeds and volunteer hosts.
- Maintain balanced plant nutrition.
- Monitor disease development during warm, humid weather.

Keeping foliage dry, improving airflow, using mulch, and reducing splash from infected debris are useful practices for tomato leaf-spot diseases. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### After Harvest
- Remove tomato plants and infected crop residue.
- Control volunteer tomatoes and susceptible weeds.
- Avoid leaving infected plant debris in or near the next tomato crop.
- Practice crop rotation where practical.
- Clean and sanitize reusable tools and equipment.
- Review field history before the next planting season.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural and sanitation practices are the main nonchemical components of target-spot management.

Useful approaches include:
- Healthy planting material.
- Removing infected leaves.
- Improving airflow.
- Reducing prolonged foliage wetness.
- Applying mulch.
- Removing crop debris.
- Controlling volunteer hosts and weeds.
- Maintaining good plant vigor.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be considered when target spot causes significant disease pressure or threatens yield.

Chemical management is generally most effective when disease is detected early and treatment begins before extensive defoliation has occurred.

Treatment decisions should consider:
- Disease severity.
- Disease history.
- Weather conditions.
- Crop growth stage.
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

Because target spot can resemble other tomato leaf-spot diseases, accurate diagnosis is important before selecting a chemical-management program. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Products should therefore be rotated according to current resistance-management guidance.

**Important:** Chemical recommendations, permitted products, formulations, application rates, resistance-management requirements, pre-harvest intervals, and regulations vary by country and can change over time. ## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Tomato cultivars differ in their susceptibility to target spot.

Where available, cultivars with improved tolerance may help reduce disease severity, but complete resistance should not be assumed.

Cultivar performance should be verified for the local production environment and *Corynespora cassiicola* population.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato target spot is suspected:

1. Inspect older and lower leaves for brown lesions.
2. Look for concentric rings and target-like patterns.
3. Check whether lesions are increasing after warm, humid weather.
4. Inspect fruit for dark, slightly sunken lesions with concentric rings.
5. Remove severely infected leaves where practical.
6. Improve airflow around the plant.
7. Avoid overhead irrigation where possible.
8. Apply mulch to reduce soil and debris splash.
9. Remove infected plant debris after harvest.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert if symptoms are difficult to distinguish from early blight or other leaf-spot diseases.

## Prevention

- Use healthy seed and transplants.
- Select suitable cultivars.
- Maintain good airflow.
- Stake or trellis tomatoes.
- Avoid excessive canopy density.
- Keep foliage as dry as practical.
- Use drip or base irrigation.
- Apply mulch to reduce splash.
- Remove infected leaves and crop debris.
- Control volunteer tomatoes and susceptible weeds.
- Practice crop rotation where practical.
- Monitor crops regularly during warm and humid conditions.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
