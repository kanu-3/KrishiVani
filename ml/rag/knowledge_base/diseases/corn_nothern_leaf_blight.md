## Symptoms

Northern leaf blight primarily affects leaves.

### Leaves
- Early lesions may appear as small, pale, gray-green, or water-soaked areas.
- Lesions enlarge into long elliptical or canoe-shaped lesions.
- Mature lesions commonly become gray, tan, or pale brown.
- Typical lesions may range from approximately 1 to 6 inches in length.
- Lesions generally run parallel to the leaf margins and veins.
- Dark gray or green fungal spores may develop in the center of mature lesions under moist conditions.
- Multiple lesions may merge and destroy large portions of the leaf.
- Severe infection can cause entire leaves to die.

### Resistant Hybrids
- Hybrids carrying specific resistance genes may develop smaller lesions.
- Lesions may appear chlorotic, narrow, or as linear streaks rather than classic cigar-shaped lesions.
- Spore production may be reduced on resistant plants.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus survives between growing seasons primarily in infected corn residue.

During favorable conditions, spores are produced on infected residue and diseased leaves.

Spores spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Air movement
- Storm systems

Disease can begin in lower leaves when spores originate from infected residue within the field. Airborne spores from surrounding fields can also introduce the pathogen into new fields.

Secondary infections can occur repeatedly when warm, humid, wet conditions persist.

The disease commonly becomes noticeable from the late vegetative stages through physiological maturity, although infection can occur earlier.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Moderate temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Heavy dew
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Dense crop canopies
- High levels of infected corn residue

Leaf wetness lasting approximately 6 hours or more can favor infection. Disease development is particularly favored by temperatures around 18–27°C (64–81°F). Hot, dry weather generally slows disease development. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

## Detection

Northern leaf blight can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop long, elliptical or cigar-shaped lesions.
- Lesions are initially gray-green and later become tan or gray.
- Lesions run approximately parallel to leaf veins and margins.
- Dark fungal spores appear in the center of mature lesions under humid conditions.
- Lesions begin on lower leaves and progress upward.
- Large numbers of lesions merge and cause extensive leaf blighting.

NCLB can sometimes resemble other corn foliar diseases. The characteristic elongated, even-edged lesions and absence of bacterial streaming or typical freckles can help distinguish it from some bacterial diseases. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Disease Severity and Impact

Northern leaf blight can cause:
- Reduced green leaf area
- Premature leaf death
- Reduced photosynthetic activity
- Reduced grain filling
- Reduced yield
- Reduced forage or silage quality under severe disease

Early infection is generally more damaging than late-season infection. Severe disease established before tasseling can result in substantial yield loss in susceptible hybrids. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select corn hybrids with improved resistance to northern leaf blight where available.
- Avoid highly susceptible hybrids in fields with a history of severe NCLB.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Consider previous disease history and residue levels when selecting hybrids.
- Obtain quality seed from reliable sources.

### During Growth
- Scout fields regularly for elongated leaf lesions.
- Pay particular attention to lower leaves early in the season.
- Monitor disease progression during moderate, wet, and humid weather.
- Identify the susceptibility level of the planted hybrid.
- Maintain balanced crop nutrition.
- Consider fungicide treatment where disease risk and crop value justify it.

### After Harvest
- Manage infected corn residue.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Consider residue reduction or incorporation practices where appropriate for the farming system.
- Avoid repeated continuous corn production in high-risk situations when practical.
- Review hybrid performance before the next planting season.

Crop rotation and residue management can reduce the amount of surviving inoculum available for subsequent crops. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Chemical Management

Foliar fungicides can be useful when northern leaf blight develops early enough to threaten yield, particularly in susceptible hybrids.

Treatment decisions should consider:
- Hybrid susceptibility.
- Disease severity.
- Rate of disease progression.
- Crop growth stage.
- Weather conditions.
- Yield potential.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

Fungicides generally provide the greatest benefit when disease is managed before extensive leaf tissue has been destroyed.

Repeated use of the same fungicide mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Products should therefore be rotated according to current resistance-management recommendations.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Corn hybrids vary considerably in susceptibility to northern leaf blight.

Two broad forms of host resistance occur:
- Race-specific resistance controlled by resistance genes such as Ht genes.
- Partial or polygenic resistance that can reduce disease development across pathogen races.

Hybrids with partial resistance may develop smaller lesions and produce fewer spores, while some race-specific resistance can provide stronger protection against compatible pathogen races. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Resistance should be confirmed for the specific hybrid and local disease environment.

## Immediate Actions

When northern leaf blight is suspected:

1. Inspect lower leaves for long, elliptical or cigar-shaped lesions.
2. Check mature lesions for dark fungal spores.
3. Monitor whether lesions are progressing toward the upper canopy.
4. Review recent temperature, rainfall, and leaf-wetness conditions.
5. Identify the planted hybrid and its resistance characteristics.
6. Assess the field history for previous NCLB.
7. Monitor upper leaves closely around tasseling and grain development.
8. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Plant northern-leaf-blight-resistant or less susceptible hybrids where available.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Manage infected crop residue.
- Avoid continuous corn production in high-risk fields when practical.
- Scout regularly during moderate, humid, wet weather.
- Monitor lower leaves early in the season.
- Maintain balanced crop nutrition.
- Consider fungicide use only when disease risk justifies treatment.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural recommendations.
