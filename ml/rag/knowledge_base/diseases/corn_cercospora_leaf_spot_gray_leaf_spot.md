# Corn Cercospora Leaf Spot Gray Leaf Spot

## Crop
Corn (Maize)

## Crop Aliases
- Corn
- Maize
- *Zea mays*
- Field corn
- Sweet corn

## Disease
Cercospora Leaf Spot / Gray Leaf Spot

## Disease Aliases
- Gray leaf spot
- Gray leaf spot of corn
- Gray leaf spot of maize
- Cercospora leaf spot
- GLS

## Scientific Name
*Cercospora zeae-maydis*

## Common Names
- Gray leaf spot
- Gray leaf spot of maize
- Cercospora leaf spot

## Hosts / Related Crops
The pathogen primarily affects:
- Maize
- Corn
- Susceptible *Zea mays* cultivars

## Symptoms

Gray leaf spot primarily affects leaves.

### Leaves
- Small tan, brown, or gray lesions may initially appear on leaves.
- Lesions gradually become elongated and rectangular.
- Mature lesions commonly have clearly defined parallel edges because they are restricted by leaf veins.
- Lesions may become gray to brown as fungal spores develop.
- Individual lesions can enlarge and merge during severe disease.
- Large portions of leaves may become necrotic.
- Severe infection can cause premature leaf death.

### Stems and Other Tissues
- The primary economic symptoms occur on foliage.
- Severe defoliation can indirectly weaken plants and reduce grain development.

## Disease Development and Spread

The pathogen survives primarily in infected maize residue left on or near the soil surface.

During favorable weather, fungal spores are produced on infected residue and newly infected leaves.

Spores can spread through:
- Wind
- Rain splash
- Air movement associated with storms
- Movement of infected crop residue

The disease can become established when spores infect susceptible leaves during periods of prolonged moisture.

Repeated cycles of infection can increase disease severity during the growing season.

Continuous maize production and poor residue management can increase the amount of pathogen inoculum available for future crops.

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is favored by:
- Warm temperatures
- High humidity
- Frequent rainfall
- Prolonged leaf wetness
- Dense crop canopies
- High levels of infected crop residue
- Continuous maize production

The disease is particularly favored when warm, humid conditions occur during the period of rapid vegetative development.

## Detection

Gray leaf spot can be suspected when:
- Leaves develop elongated rectangular lesions.
- Lesions are tan, brown, gray, or gray-brown.
- Lesion edges are generally parallel to leaf veins.
- Lesions increase rapidly in number or size after warm, humid weather.
- Lesions merge and cause large areas of leaf necrosis.
- Disease is concentrated in lower foliage and progresses upward under favorable conditions.

## Disease Severity and Impact

Gray leaf spot can cause:
- Premature leaf death
- Reduced photosynthetic activity
- Reduced grain filling
- Lower grain yield
- Reduced plant vigor
- Increased risk of lodging when severe defoliation occurs

Disease severity is particularly important when upper leaves are infected before or during grain filling because these leaves contribute substantially to carbohydrate production for developing grain.

## Management

### Before Planting
- Select corn hybrids with improved resistance to gray leaf spot where available.
- Avoid planting highly susceptible hybrids in fields with a history of severe disease.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Consider field history and residue levels when selecting management strategies.
- Obtain quality seed from reliable sources.

### During Growth
- Scout fields regularly for leaf lesions.
- Pay particular attention to lower leaves and fields with a history of gray leaf spot.
- Monitor disease development during warm and humid weather.
- Maintain balanced crop nutrition.
- Use integrated disease-management practices based on disease risk and local recommendations.

### After Harvest
- Manage infected crop residue.
- Avoid unnecessary continuous maize production where disease pressure is high.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Incorporate or otherwise manage residue according to locally appropriate conservation and disease-management practices.
- Review field history before the next maize crop.

Residue management and crop rotation can reduce the amount of inoculum available for subsequent crops.

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Crop rotation where practical.
- Selection of resistant or less susceptible hybrids.
- Managing infected crop residue.
- Avoiding continuous maize production in high-risk fields.
- Monitoring fields regularly.
- Maintaining balanced plant nutrition.
- Considering field history when choosing hybrids and disease-management strategies.

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural management is the primary nonchemical approach.

Reducing infected residue and avoiding continuous maize production can lower pathogen inoculum between cropping cycles.

Planting resistant hybrids is another important component of integrated disease management.

Specific biological-control products are not included in this initial record because sufficient evidence was not established for a specific recommendation.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be used in fields where gray leaf spot poses significant yield risk, particularly when susceptible hybrids are grown and disease develops before or around the critical grain-filling period.

Fungicide decisions should consider:
- Hybrid susceptibility
- Disease severity
- Disease progression
- Crop growth stage
- Weather conditions
- Yield potential
- Local extension recommendations

Preventive or early disease management is generally more effective than attempting to control severe established defoliation.

Repeated use of fungicides with the same mode of action can contribute to fungicide resistance. Fungicides should therefore be selected and rotated according to current resistance-management guidance.

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Corn hybrids vary considerably in susceptibility to gray leaf spot.

Using hybrids with improved genetic resistance is one of the most important management strategies, particularly in fields with:
- A history of severe gray leaf spot.
- Continuous maize production.
- High levels of infected residue.
- Warm and humid growing conditions.

Resistance should be confirmed for the specific hybrid and local disease environment.

## Immediate Actions

When gray leaf spot is suspected:

1. Inspect lower leaves for elongated rectangular lesions.
2. Check whether lesions are restricted by leaf veins.
3. Monitor whether lesions are progressing upward through the canopy.
4. Review recent weather conditions for warm, humid, wet periods.
5. Assess field history for previous gray leaf spot.
6. Identify the planted hybrid and its susceptibility level.
7. Monitor upper leaves closely during grain development.
8. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Plant gray-leaf-spot-resistant or less susceptible hybrids where available.
- Use crop rotation where practical.
- Manage infected crop residue.
- Avoid continuous maize production in high-risk fields when practical.
- Scout fields regularly.
- Monitor weather conditions that favor disease development.
- Maintain balanced crop nutrition.
- Use fungicides only when justified by disease risk and current local recommendations.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
