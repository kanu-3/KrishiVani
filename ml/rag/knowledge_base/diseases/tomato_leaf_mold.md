## Symptoms

Tomato leaf mold primarily affects foliage but can occasionally affect flowers and fruit.

### Leaves
- Small pale green to yellow spots may initially appear on the upper surface of leaves.
- Spots usually have indistinct margins.
- Corresponding olive-green to brown velvety fungal growth develops on the underside of affected leaves.
- As lesions enlarge, adjacent spots may merge.
- Infected leaves gradually turn brown and wither.
- Severely affected leaves may die but often remain attached to the plant.
- Older leaves are generally affected before younger foliage.

### Flowers
- Infected blossoms may turn brown or black.
- Severely affected flowers may dry and fall from the plant.
- Blossom infection can reduce fruit set.

### Fruit
- Fruit infection is less common than leaf infection.
- Infected fruit may develop a smooth, irregular dark area, often near the stem attachment.
- The affected tissue may become sunken, dry, and leathery.
- Severe fruit infection can reduce fruit quality and marketability.

## Disease Development and Spread

The fungus can survive in:
- Infected crop residue
- Infected tomato seed
- Infected plant material
- Potentially contaminated greenhouse or high-tunnel surfaces and equipment

The pathogen produces spores primarily on the underside of infected leaves.

Spores can spread through:
- Wind
- Air movement
- Rain splash
- Overhead irrigation
- Contaminated tools
- Workers handling infected plants
- Potentially insects

The disease can complete multiple infection cycles during one growing season when humidity remains high. New spores can form on infected leaves within approximately 10–12 days under favorable conditions. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

## Favorable Conditions

Disease development is strongly favored by:
- Relative humidity above approximately 85%
- High humidity in greenhouses and high tunnels
- Poor air circulation
- Dense foliage
- Prolonged periods of humid conditions
- Moderate temperatures

Optimal disease development occurs around 71–75°F (22–24°C), although infection can occur over a broader temperature range. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Tomato leaf mold is therefore particularly important in:
- Greenhouses
- High tunnels
- Enclosed production systems
- Dense, poorly ventilated tomato canopies

## Detection

Tomato leaf mold can be suspected when:
- Older leaves develop small pale yellow or greenish-yellow spots on the upper surface.
- Corresponding olive-green to brown velvety mold appears on the lower leaf surface.
- Leaves progressively turn brown and die.
- Dead leaves remain attached to the plant.
- Symptoms are concentrated in environments with very high humidity.
- Blossoms become dark and fall.
- Fruit develops smooth dark lesions around the stem end in severe cases.

The combination of yellow upper-leaf spots and olive-colored fungal growth on the underside is particularly characteristic of tomato leaf mold. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

## Disease Severity and Impact

Tomato leaf mold can cause:
- Progressive leaf yellowing
- Leaf necrosis
- Premature or progressive leaf death
- Reduced photosynthetic capacity
- Reduced plant vigor
- Reduced fruit production when severe
- Blossom loss
- Fruit infection in severe cases
- Reduced yield and fruit quality

Severe disease can result in extensive loss of foliage and indirectly reduce yield because fewer healthy leaves remain to support fruit development. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

## Management

### Before Planting
- Use healthy tomato seed and transplants from reliable sources.
- Consider cultivars with resistance to locally prevalent leaf-mold races where available.
- Avoid introducing infected plant material into greenhouses and high tunnels.
- Clean greenhouse and high-tunnel production areas before planting.
- Sanitize stakes, ties, trellises, benches, and tools where practical.
- Provide adequate plant spacing to improve air movement.

### During Growth
- Scout regularly, especially during periods of relative humidity above 85%.
- Improve ventilation in greenhouses and high tunnels.
- Use fans and vents where appropriate.
- Space plants adequately.
- Stake, string, or prune plants to improve airflow.
- Prefer drip irrigation and avoid wetting the foliage.
- Remove severely infected leaves or plant material where practical.
- Avoid excessive canopy density.
- Monitor humidity and condensation.
- Work with plants when foliage is dry to reduce mechanical spread.

### After Harvest
- Remove tomato crop residue from the production area.
- Clean and sanitize greenhouse or high-tunnel structures and production surfaces.
- Remove infected leaves and plant debris.
- Sanitize reusable stakes, ties, trellises, tools, and other equipment.
- Avoid carrying contaminated material into the next crop.

Removing crop residue is particularly important because the pathogen can survive in infected plant debris and produce new spores when environmental conditions become favorable. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

## Cultural Practices

Important cultural practices include:
- Starting with healthy seed and transplants.
- Maintaining low relative humidity where practical.
- Improving ventilation.
- Using fans in enclosed production.
- Maintaining adequate plant spacing.
- Staking, stringing, or pruning plants to improve airflow.
- Using drip irrigation.
- Avoiding unnecessary foliage wetness.
- Removing infected leaves.
- Sanitizing tools and production equipment.
- Removing crop residue at the end of the season.

Reducing humidity is one of the most important management strategies for tomato leaf mold. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

## Biological / Organic Management

Cultural and environmental management are the main nonchemical approaches.

Useful practices include:
- Reducing relative humidity.
- Improving ventilation.
- Increasing air circulation.
- Removing infected leaves.
- Maintaining adequate plant spacing.
- Using healthy planting material.
- Sanitizing production equipment.
- Removing infected crop residue.

## Chemical Management

Fungicides may be used when tomato leaf mold causes significant disease pressure, particularly in greenhouse and high-tunnel production.

Applications are generally most effective when made preventively or before extensive infection develops. Product selection and timing should be based on:
- Disease risk.
- Humidity conditions.
- Crop growth stage.
- Cultivar susceptibility.
- Current local agricultural recommendations.

Fungicide applications should follow product-label requirements, particularly for greenhouse or high-tunnel use.

Repeated use of products from the same fungicide mode-of-action group can contribute to fungicide resistance. Fungicide groups should therefore be rotated according to current resistance-management recommendations. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

For organic production, some copper-based products may provide suppression, but efficacy and permitted uses vary by product and jurisdiction. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

## Resistant / Tolerant Varieties

Many tomato varieties are available with resistance designations for leaf mold.

However, *Passalora fulva* has multiple races, and resistance in a cultivar may protect against only some pathogen races. A variety described as resistant may therefore perform differently depending on the local pathogen population. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

Where leaf mold is a recurring problem:
- Prefer varieties with documented resistance to locally prevalent races.
- Test resistant varieties on a limited scale when their effectiveness in the local production environment is uncertain.
- Do not rely on resistance alone; combine cultivar selection with humidity and sanitation management.

## Immediate Actions

When tomato leaf mold is suspected:

1. Inspect the upper leaf surface for small pale yellow or greenish-yellow spots.
2. Check the corresponding underside of the leaf for olive-green or brown velvety growth.
3. Inspect older leaves first because they are commonly affected before younger leaves.
4. Measure or assess greenhouse/high-tunnel humidity if applicable.
5. Improve ventilation and air circulation immediately.
6. Avoid overhead irrigation.
7. Remove severely infected leaves where practical.
8. Sanitize tools and equipment used around infected plants.
9. Remove infected crop residue after the production cycle.
10. Consult a local agricultural extension or plant-health expert for confirmation and treatment decisions.

## Prevention

- Use healthy seed and transplants.
- Select cultivars with appropriate leaf-mold resistance where available.
- Maintain relative humidity below levels that favor disease whenever practical.
- Improve greenhouse and high-tunnel ventilation.
- Use fans and vents to increase air movement.
- Maintain adequate plant spacing.
- Stake, string, or prune plants appropriately.
- Use drip irrigation.
- Avoid unnecessary foliage wetness.
- Remove infected leaves promptly.
- Sanitize tools, stakes, ties, trellises, and production surfaces.
- Remove tomato crop residue after harvest.
- Rotate fungicide modes of action when chemical control is required.
- Follow current local agricultural and pesticide recommendations.
